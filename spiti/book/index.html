Before you start

How to use this

This is not a guidebook. It won’t tell you where to eat or when to leave. It is the other thing — the layer of history, geology, myth and old travel writing that sits underneath the road you are driving on, arranged in the order you will meet it.

Each chapter matches a stop or a leg. Read the chapter the night before, or in the car on the way. Nothing here needs to be read in sequence, but the book does have an argument, and the argument runs roughly like this:

The road you are on is one of the oldest arguments in Asia. It is the line where the Indian subcontinent physically ran into Asia, and it is also the line where India met Tibet, where Hinduism met Buddhism, where the Mughals met the Mongols, where the British met the Chinese. All of these collisions happened in the same 400 kilometres. Some of them are still happening.

A few recurring boxes:

  • In the rock — what you are looking at, geologically
  • The other story — the myth version, told as myth
  • Who came through here — the surveyors, spies, monks and writers who did this before you

One request before you start. In Chapter 11 you will read about the fossils of Langza. Please don’t buy any. The reasons are in that chapter.

One thing to notice each day

If you read nothing else, carry these.

Day 1 · Shimla → Sarahan. The memorial at Jeori to the 122 men who died building this road. And, at Kotgarh, that the entire Himachal apple economy began with saplings one American planted about a century ago.

Day 2 · → Chitkul. The walls. Stone course, timber course, stone course, no mortar — the kath-kuni technique that survived the 1905 earthquake. Once you see it you cannot stop seeing it.

Day 3 · → Kalpa. Kinner Kailash at dawn, and the fact that Shiva’s winter residence and Padmasambhava’s footprint are eighty kilometres apart on the same road.

Day 4 · → Tabo. The exact stretch, somewhere past Nako, where the trees stop. You are crossing out of monsoon India. Also: a mud building from 996 CE that has never once closed.

Day 5 · → Mud. You are sleeping in the only valley on earth where the buchen exist.

Day 6 · → Kaza. Ammonites weathering out of a hillside at 4,400 metres. That hillside was the floor of an ocean. Don’t buy one.

Day 7 · → Losar. The Chicham bridge, and the thousand-foot gap that until 2017 was crossed by hauling yourself across on a wire.

Day 8 · → Chandratal. Kunzum La is the watershed: on one side the water goes to the Sutlej, on the other to the Chenab. And at the lake, the story of a man who refused heaven rather than abandon a dog.

Day 9 · → Manali. Three thousand metres down in a day. Watch for the exact point where the trees come back.

Chapter 0

Deep Time

What the road is actually cutting through

Start with a fact that takes a moment to absorb. Everything you will drive through between Tabo and Kunzum La — the ochre cliffs, the grey shale, the whole enormous dry emptiness — was once the floor of a sea.

Not metaphorically. Literally the seabed.

For most of the Mesozoic, the landmass now called India was an island, drifting north across an ocean that geologists call the Tethys. Off its northern shore lay a broad, shallow continental shelf, the kind of warm carbonate platform you would find today off northern Australia. Things lived there: ammonites in coiled shells, belemnites, oysters, brachiopods, corals. They died, sank, and were buried in black mud. Over a hundred million years the mud compacted into rock. Geologists call one of those layers the Spiti Shale, and beneath it the Tagling limestone, both laid down in the Jurassic, roughly 150 to 200 million years ago.

Then India hit Asia.

The collision began around 50 million years ago, and it has not stopped. The Indian plate is still pushing north, and the mountains are still rising. The seabed was not destroyed in the collision — it was lifted, folded, and stacked, and it is now sitting at 4,400 metres above the sea it was deposited in. At Langza, children pick ammonites out of the hillside. Nothing on this road is more astonishing than that, and most people drive past it without noticing.

This is why the two halves of your trip look so different.

Kinnaur (Days 1–4) is on the southern flank of the Great Himalaya. It gets the monsoon, because the monsoon gets that far and no further. So: deodar forest, apple orchards, waterfalls, green. It is also structurally chaotic — sheared, faulted rock, over-steep slopes, and a river that has cut a gorge faster than the slopes can adjust. This is why Kinnaur slides. The landslides at Nigulsari and Chaura are not bad luck, they are the mountain doing exactly what the geology says it should do.

Spiti (Days 4 onward) is on the other side of the Himalayan crest, in what geographers call the Trans-Himalaya or the Tethyan Himalaya. The monsoon does not get here. It is a cold desert: under 200 mm of precipitation a year, most of it snow. Nothing grows unless someone irrigates it. What you see is the raw stratigraphy, unvegetated, with each layer legible from the road like the pages of a book seen edge-on.

The line between these two worlds is somewhere around Nako. You will feel it as a change in the air — the humidity drops away, your lips start to crack, and the colours go from green to brown, ochre, grey and violet.

In the rock

How to read a Spiti hillside

Once you are past Sumdo, try this. Pick a big hillside and look for:

  • Horizontal banding — the original seabed layers, undeformed. You are seeing 30 million years of deposition in one glance.
  • Folded or tilted banding — the same layers, crumpled by the collision. If the bands run diagonally or in visible arcs, you are looking at the Indian plate being squeezed.
  • Scree fans at the base of every slope — the mountains actively falling apart. In a wet climate these would be overgrown within a decade. Here they just sit.
  • Flat terraces well above the river — old valley floors, from when the river ran higher. The river has since cut down through its own deposits. In places around Kaza these terraces are hundreds of metres thick, which is where the villages and fields are.

The rivers, which explain the whole route

Your route is really a tour of the Sutlej and then the Chandra, and the difference between them is the point.

The Sutlej is older than the Himalaya. It rises near Mount Kailash in Tibet, on the far side of the main range, and then does something impossible: it cuts straight through the Himalaya to reach the plains. It could only do that if it was already flowing there before the mountains rose, sawing downward as they lifted around it. Rivers that do this are called antecedent. You are driving up an antecedent gorge from Shimla to Khab, which is why the walls are so sheer and the road so improbable.

At Khab, past Pooh, the Sutlej is joined by the Spiti river. From there you follow the Spiti upstream, deeper into the cold desert, to Kaza and Losar.

Then at Kunzum La you cross the watershed, and everything changes direction. On the far side, water flows into the Chandra, which runs west, meets the Bhaga at Tandi, becomes the Chandrabhaga, and eventually becomes the Chenab — one of the five rivers of Punjab and a river of Pakistan. At Chandratal you are camped beside the Chandra’s headwaters.

So: Days 1–7 you are in the Indus basin, drainage to Pakistan via the Sutlej. Day 8 you cross a pass and you are in the Chenab basin, drainage to Pakistan via a different river. Two of the great rivers of the Punjab begin within a day’s drive of each other, and the only thing separating them is Kunzum La.

Interlude One

The People of the Road

Read this before Day 1. Everything else in the book is scenery without it.

The trouble with a book like this is that it is very easy to write about a valley as though it were a collection of monasteries, passes and geological formations with some people incidentally standing around in it. That gets it backwards. The monasteries exist because people fed the monks. The terraces exist because somebody carried the soil. The road exists because somebody was made to build it.

So before the road starts, here is who lives on it.

Who they are

There is no single people on this route. There are at least four overlapping ones, and the transitions between them are gradual rather than sharp.

The Pahari belt (Shimla to Rampur). Hindi- and Pahari-speaking, overwhelmingly Hindu, organised around village deities. Agriculturally this is the apple belt now, and was a grain-and-livestock economy before.

The Kinnauras (Kinnaur, Days 2–4). A composite people, officially classified as a Scheduled Tribe. The old anthropological account layers them: Kiratas (the archaic Himalayan peoples of Sanskrit literature), Khasas, Indo-Aryan arrivals, and Bhotia populations in the upper valleys with Tibetan linguistic and cultural links. Kinnaur is not one linguistic community but several: Kinnauri in the lower valleys, Bhoti Kinnauri in the upper ones, and in Chitkul a language of its own, Chitkuli Kinnauri, spoken by a few thousand people and nowhere else on earth.

The Spitians (Days 4–8). Culturally, linguistically and religiously Tibetan. They speak Spiti Bhoti, a Tibetic language, write in Tibetan script, and their religious world is the western Tibetan Buddhist one. The border with China runs a short distance away and has been closed since 1962, which means these are Tibetans on the Indian side of a line drawn in 1684 and hardened in the twentieth century.

The Lahaulis (Day 9). Between the two worlds again: Buddhist in the upper valleys, Hindu-Buddhist mixed lower down, with their own languages (Bhoti, Manchad, Tinan, Bunan) and their own presiding mountain god, Gyephang, whose peak you will see from the Lahaul side of the tunnel.

Both Kinnaur and Lahaul-Spiti are Scheduled Tribe districts, which has real consequences: land in these districts cannot generally be bought by outsiders. When you notice that no chain hotels have appeared in Spiti, and that almost everything is a homestay or a locally owned guesthouse, that is why. It is one of the few places in India where the tourism boom has been structurally forced to stay in local hands.

The thing nobody tells you: caste is here too

Kinnauri society was, and to a degree still is, stratified. The dominant agricultural community are the Kanets (locally Rajput or Khasiya), who held the land. Below them sat a group of artisan and service communities — variously Koli, Chamang, Domang, Badhi, Lohar — who were the blacksmiths, the leatherworkers, the weavers, the carpenters and, importantly, the musicians.

That last one matters. At every temple festival, every deity procession, every wedding you might see on this road, the drums and the long horns are being played by people from the communities that were, until recently, not permitted to enter the temple whose god they were summoning. The music that makes the deity’s presence possible was the hereditary labour of people the deity’s shrine excluded.

Untouchability was formally abolished in 1950 and things have changed substantially. But temple entry disputes have occurred in Himachal within living memory, and the Scheduled Caste share of the population in Kinnaur is around a quarter — far from marginal. If you find yourself charmed by the deity system, hold both facts at once. It is a genuinely remarkable form of village democracy, and it was built on a hierarchy.

Spiti’s social structure is different — Tibetan Buddhist rather than caste Hindu — but not flat either. The Nono families were aristocracy. There were landholding households, tenant households, and hereditary low-status occupational groups including the beda, musicians again, and blacksmiths, who carried stigma in Tibetan societies much as they did in Hindu ones. The pattern of “the people who make the music are the people you don’t marry” turns out to cross religious boundaries.

The household, and why it is shaped like that

Almost everything unusual about social organisation in these valleys comes down to one problem: there is not enough land, and it must not be divided.

A Kinnauri or Spitian family holds a few terraced fields. Under ordinary partible inheritance, those fields halve every generation, and within three generations nobody can feed themselves. Every society in the high Himalaya independently solved this in the same three ways.

One: impartible inheritance. The land passes undivided, usually to the eldest son. The house, the fields and the name stay together.

Two: polyandry. If the brothers all marry the same woman, there is one household, one set of heirs, and one holding. Fraternal polyandry was widespread across Kinnaur, Spiti, Ladakh and Tibet, and this is why. It is now uncommon and declining, replaced by monogamy as the cash economy and outside employment have made land less decisive.

The version told locally is nicer: Draupadi and the Pandavas, who are said to have spent part of their exile here. Ask about polyandry in Kinnaur and you will usually get the Mahabharata before you get the economics. Both explanations are true in their own way — one describes the mechanism, the other describes how a community made sense of itself.

Three: the monastery. In Spiti, the classic pattern sent a younger son to the gompa. He got an education, a livelihood and status; the family got religious merit and one fewer claimant on the land. The monastery got a continuous supply of monks. This is the economic engine underneath every whitewashed building you will photograph on this trip: the monasteries were not separate from the household system, they were part of it.

Daughters had a parallel route. The chomo — women who take vows but often live at or near home rather than in a formal nunnery — occupied an unusual position: religiously committed, economically productive, not married, not quite in the world and not quite out of it. Spiti has an active nunnery tradition and the chomo are a real presence in village life.

All of which means: when you meet a family in Mud or Kibber and they mention a brother who is a monk at Ki, you are not hearing about a personal spiritual calling so much as about a thousand-year-old system of land management that also happens to have produced some of the finest art in Asia.

Begar: the grievance that made Himachal

This is the people’s history of the road, and it is the part the road does not commemorate.

Begar means forced, unpaid labour. In the Shimla hill states it was the basic mechanism by which the rajas — and then the British — got anything done, because there was very little cash and a great deal of mountain.

It took several forms. Road begar was compulsory porterage: villagers made to carry loads on their backs along mountain tracks, without pay. Building materials. Supplies. Furniture. Government files. The luggage of officials. Whatever needed to go up, a hill man carried. Beyond that there was agricultural and domestic labour owed to chiefs and jagirdars — working their land, staffing their households, providing for their ceremonies. Contemporary administrative writing is quite open that the customary ceremonies of the hill states could not have been performed without it.

When the British established supremacy over the hill rulers, they did not abolish begar. They recognised and institutionalised it, because it was extremely convenient. The Shimla summer capital, that whole absurd annual migration of a government up a hill, ran substantially on the backs of conscripted hill men.

Related obligations ran alongside it: bethu, unpaid service to a landlord in exchange for a homestead; and reet, a custom under which a woman could effectively be purchased for marriage, with the state taking a fee.

Lord Dalhousie’s supposed sympathy for the porters of the hill roads — one of the two reasons given for commissioning the Hindustan–Tibet Road in 1850 — should be read against this. He was troubled by the men carrying loads. The road they got was built by conscripted labour and used to move more loads more efficiently.

The revolt

Resistance was continuous and local, and it eventually became the freedom movement of the hills.

The pattern repeats across the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries: Chamba in 1895, against forced labour and the exactions of the raja’s wazir; Beja and Theog in 1898; Baghal from 1897; Suket in 1924, led by Ratan Singh of Banaik, against forced labour and excessive taxes. In Bushahr — your kingdom, Days 1 to 4 — the Dumha movement centred on Rohru was a collective non-cooperation against cash land taxes, in which peasants took their children and cattle into the forest and simply left, refusing to return until their demands were met. They won.

In 1920, Satyanand Stokes — the apple man from Kotgarh — and Babu Kanshi Ram of Kunihar launched a movement to end begar in the Shimla hills. This is the same man from Chapter 2, and it is the more important half of his story. An American who had come to convert people ended up organising them against a system the British had institutionalised, and was jailed for it.

The Praja Mandal movements followed in the 1930s and 1940s — people’s associations demanding responsible government in the princely states. Their standing charter of demands was almost identical everywhere: civil liberties, abolition of begar, a large cut in land revenue, recognition of the Praja Mandal, and responsible government. Bushahr’s own Praja Mandal became effective from 1945.

At Dhami in 1939, a Praja Mandal procession presenting exactly those demands was fired on. It was the first firing tragedy in what is now Himachal, and it drew national attention — Gandhi and Nehru both took it up.

After independence, the Suket Satyagraha of February 1948 broke the princely states within a week. Ruler after ruler signed merger agreements. On 15 April 1948 the Chief Commissioner’s Province of Himachal Pradesh came into existence out of some thirty princely states, and Bushahr — Kamru, Sarahan, Rampur, a dynasty claiming a hundred and ten kings back to Krishna’s son — ceased to be a state.

And begar was abolished, bethu regulated, and reet banned.

Himachal became a Union Territory in 1956 and a full state on 25 January 1971, under Y. S. Parmar, the doctor of law from Sirmaur who had led the Praja Mandal movement and became its first chief minister.

So when you drive this road, hold two things together. It is a spectacular piece of nineteenth-century engineering. It was also, for the people who live beside it, an instrument of extraction before it was anything else, and the campaign to end that extraction is one of the founding stories of the state you are driving through.

The village god

The other thing you need before Day 1 is the deota system, because it is the actual government of these valleys in a way that is hard to overstate.

Every village, and often every hamlet, has a deity. The deity has a physical form — usually a rath, a palanquin of carved wood and precious metal hung with masks and cloth, carried on poles by a team of men. The deity has a staff: a gur (oracle, who is possessed and speaks for the god), a pujari, musicians, a treasurer, and a village committee.

And the deity has powers. Real ones. Village deities in Himachal settle disputes, approve or forbid marriages, decide when the sowing begins, adjudicate land quarrels, bless or refuse new construction, and travel to each other’s festivals to confer. Deities have opinions on roads, dams, mobile towers and outsiders. In several documented cases in Himachal, development projects have been stalled or modified because a deity, speaking through a gur in front of the whole village, said no.

This is not folklore performed for tourists. It is a parallel legal and political system, several thousand years older than the district administration, and it is generally obeyed.

When you see men carrying a decorated palanquin at a jostling, rocking half-run, with long horns and drums, you are watching a god travel. Stand aside, don’t block the path, don’t get in front for the photograph, and if the rath rocks violently, understand that the god is answering a question.

On Day 2 at Kamru and Day 3 at Kalpa you are in deota country. On Day 9 in the Kullu valley you will be in the densest concentration of it in the world, where several hundred deities gather annually for Dussehra. It runs the whole length of the road.

Language: the least you can carry

Nobody expects you to speak Kinnauri or Bhoti. But a couple of words used correctly changes the temperature of an encounter completely.

In Spiti and upper Kinnaur (Buddhist areas):

  • Juley (JOO-lay) — hello, goodbye, thank you, please. A universal Ladakhi-Tibetan courtesy, understood everywhere up here. Use it constantly.
  • Tashi delek — a warmer, more formal greeting; auspicious wishes.
  • Thugje che — thank you.

In Kinnaur (Hindu areas) and everywhere:

  • Namaste works. Elders may be greeted with a slight bow.

Everywhere:

  • Gompa — monastery. Lama — monk or teacher. Chorten — stupa. Mani wall — a wall of carved prayer stones. La — a pass (so “Kunzum La” already means “Kunzum Pass”; “Kunzum La Pass” is saying it twice).
  • Nono — the hereditary ruler of Spiti. Gur — an oracle. Rath — a deity’s palanquin.
  • Chhang — barley beer. Arak — the distilled version. Tsampa — roasted barley flour, the staple.

One usage note. People here are not “tribals” in conversation, they are Kinnauri or Spitian. And Spiti is not “Tibet” — saying so to a Spitian is like calling an Irishman English. They are Indian citizens with a Tibetan cultural inheritance, and for many families the distinction has been politically consequential since 1962.

Sidebar

A Note on Women

Short, because it is easy to drive through this landscape and register only the men.

The high Himalaya has a reputation, largely deserved, for giving women more room than the plains. Purdah is absent. Women work in the fields alongside men and often run the household economy. Polyandry, whatever else it was, gave a woman one household rather than being absorbed into a competing one, and Himalayan women have historically had property rights, divorce rights and remarriage rights that women in much of northern India did not.

The work, though, is enormous. In a Spiti or Kinnauri household the fodder, the water, the fuel, the dung collection, the cooking, the child care, the weaving and a large share of the field labour are women’s, and the day is long. The phrase field researchers use is that mechanisation and cash cropping reduced men’s labour considerably more than women’s.

Two institutions are worth knowing about.

The chomo. Women who take religious vows in Spiti often do not enter a large formal institution the way monks do. Historically many lived at or near the family home, contributing labour, practising, and occupying a category that was neither married nor cloistered. This gave some women a real alternative to marriage in a system with very few. There are now proper nunneries in Spiti, and the position of ordained women in Tibetan Buddhism — long structurally unequal to that of monks in terms of ordination and education — has been changing significantly in the exile community.

Reet. The custom, current in the hill states into the twentieth century, by which a woman could effectively be transferred for a payment, with the state taking a fee. It was one of the things the Praja Mandal movement and the new state targeted, and it was banned after 1948. When you read about the abolition of begar, note that reet went with it. They were part of the same package of arrangements.

Today, Himachal has among the better social indicators in India — female literacy, health, school enrolment — and Kinnaur and Lahaul-Spiti do well within it. Kinnaur’s recorded sex ratio, though, is low, and that is its own uncomfortable subject.

If you are in a homestay, the person running it is very likely a woman, and the income from it very likely goes to her directly rather than through a man. That is one of the quieter and better effects of the homestay model.

Chapter 1

Shimla, and the Idea of a Road

Day 1 · 16 September · departure

The town that ran an empire on a ridge

Shimla makes very little sense as a capital city. It sits on a narrow ridge at 2,200 metres, fifty miles from the nearest railway of the period, reachable only by a road that a cart could barely use. Yet from the 1860s to 1947, for several months every year, the government of British India packed itself into carts and moved here — the Viceroy, the Commander-in-Chief, the departments, the files, the clerks, the whole apparatus of a state governing a fifth of humanity, hauled up a hill to escape the heat.

Nothing about the place is accidental. The Mall, the Gaiety Theatre, the Anglican church, the half-timbered cottages, Viceregal Lodge with its grey Scottish stone: this is a small English town built at altitude by people who missed home and had the resources to manufacture it. Kipling worked here as a journalist and set a great deal of his early fiction here — Plain Tales from the Hills is essentially Shimla gossip, transcribed and sharpened, and its subject is the peculiar social hothouse that formed when a few hundred bored, powerful people were confined to one ridge for a season.

But Shimla is not where your road starts. Your road starts with a decision made in Shimla.

The Great Hindustan–Tibet Road

In June 1850, Lord Dalhousie, then Governor-General, ordered work to begin on a road from the plains to the Tibetan frontier. It was styled the Great Hindustan–Tibet Road, and the ambition was extraordinary: arguably the first major highway commissioned in India since Sher Shah Suri’s Grand Trunk Road three centuries before, and certainly the first serious mountain highway in this part of the world.

Two reasons are usually given, and both are probably true.

The official reason was trade with Tibet. The commodities that came down from the Tibetan plateau were valuable and light: pashmina wool, borax, musk, salt, gold dust, semi-precious stones. Going the other way went grain, cloth, sugar, and eventually tea. Whoever controlled the passes controlled the margin. The British wanted it.

The stated reason was more sympathetic, and slightly implausible. Dalhousie is said to have been appalled by begar, the system of forced unpaid labour under which hill people were conscripted to carry loads — timber, supplies, government files — up to Shimla on their backs. He wanted, the story goes, to build a proper road so the men who trudged it would suffer less. Whether or not he meant it, the road was built largely by exactly the kind of labour he claimed to pity.

The Commander-in-Chief, Sir Charles Napier, worked on the alignment. East India Company resources were thrown at it. From Shimla to Shipki La on the Tibet border came to about 228 miles.

The early trace went Shimla → Fagu → Theog → Matiana → Narkanda → Kotgarh → Rampur → Sarahan → Nichar → Wangtu → Chini (now Kalpa) → Rarang → Akpa → Jangi, and from Jangi onward it degraded into a rough track through Labrang, Sugnam and Namgia to the pass itself. In those days the road took the high spurs. Today it mostly follows the valley floors, which is why you will be driving alongside the Sutlej rather than above it.

Two things worth carrying with you as you drive:

The road was never really finished, and never will be. It has been under continuous construction for 176 years. What you will see — the tracked excavators parked in laybys, the shuttered stretches, the men with shovels — is not maintenance, it is the ongoing condition of the road.

People died building it, and are named. At Jeori, where the fork climbs to Sarahan, there is a memorial to 122 men who died in the construction of this road. You will pass it on Day 1. It is easy to miss.

Who came through here

Alexander Gerard

Before the road there were the surveyors, and the most important of them for this route were the Gerard brothers — Alexander, James and Patrick — Scottish officers in Company service who spent the 1810s and 1820s walking Kinnaur with barometers and theodolites. Alexander Gerard went to Shipki La and mapped the Sutlej gorge in a way nobody had before. His measurements of altitude were the first reliable ones for this region, and his accounts fed directly into the British realisation that the passes were crossable, the trade was real, and the frontier was closer than they thought.

Everything in this book that happens after 1850 happens because the Gerards walked it first.

How the move actually worked

It is worth pausing on the logistics, because they are insane.

Twice a year, the government of British India relocated. Not symbolically. The Viceroy, the Commander-in-Chief, the Foreign Department, the Army Department, the Home Department, the secretariat, the clerks, the records, the treasury and the printing presses all went up to Shimla around March and came back down to Calcutta — and after 1911, Delhi — around October.

The files went in boxes on the backs of men. The furniture went the same way. Before the Kalka–Shimla railway opened in 1903 this meant carts to Kalka and then a tonga road, and before the tonga road, porters the whole way. Estimates of the number of people involved in supporting the annual migration run into the many thousands.

The system that supplied those porters was begar (Interlude One). This is the part the Mall does not mention.

Shimla was also, for several months a year, the effective capital of a fifth of humanity — arguably making it the only hill station in history to govern a subcontinent. Decisions taken on this ridge determined policy in Burma, Aden, the Persian Gulf and Tibet.

The Kalka–Shimla railway, and Baba Bhalku

The narrow-gauge railway from Kalka opened in 1903: 96 kilometres, over 800 bridges, and more than a hundred tunnels. It is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site as part of the Mountain Railways of India.

Attached to it is the story of Baba Bhalku, a local man from the Solan area, illiterate and by tradition mystically gifted, who is credited in Himachali memory with guiding the British engineers through terrain that had defeated them — reputedly by walking the alignment with a stick and indicating where the tunnels should go. There is a railway museum named after him in Shimla.

How much of this is history and how much is folk correction of a colonial record that erased Indian contributions is impossible to untangle, and the ambiguity is itself the point. The record we have is the engineers’. The memory that survives locally is Bhalku’s.

Two conferences

Shimla appears twice in the political history of modern South Asia, both times in ways that shaped the region you are about to drive through.

The Simla Conference, 1945. Viceroy Wavell convened the Congress and the Muslim League at Viceregal Lodge to try to agree an interim government. It collapsed over the question of who could nominate Muslim members. Most historians treat its failure as one of the decisive steps towards Partition. You can walk through the room.

The Simla Agreement, 1972. Indira Gandhi and Zulfikar Ali Bhutto met here after the 1971 war and signed the agreement that converted the Kashmir ceasefire line into the Line of Control and committed both sides to resolving disputes bilaterally. It remains the reference document for India–Pakistan relations, and it was signed on this ridge.

Scandal Point, and the social machine

At the junction of the Mall and the Ridge is a spot called Scandal Point. The story attached to it is that a Maharaja of Patiala eloped from here with the daughter of a British Commander-in-Chief, and was subsequently barred from Shimla — a story with several versions and not much documentation.

Whether or not it happened, the name tells you what Shimla was: a hothouse of a few hundred powerful, bored people confined to one ridge for a season, in which reputation was the currency and gossip was the market. This is Kipling’s raw material. Plain Tales from the Hills is, essentially, field notes.

If you have a spare hour before leaving, Viceregal Lodge (now the Indian Institute of Advanced Study) is genuinely worth it, and the Gaiety Theatre on the Mall, restored, has been in continuous use since 1887.

Chapter 2

Narkanda, Kotgarh, Rampur

Day 1 · 16 September · Shimla → Sarahan, roughly 175 km

The first two hours

For the first stretch out of Shimla you are in deodar forest, and this is the part of the drive that looks most like the Himalaya of the imagination — dark cedar, rhododendron, hill villages with slate roofs and carved wooden balconies. This is also the greenest you will see for the next eight days. Take a proper look.

At Narkanda (2,700 m) the road crosses a ridge and the view opens north to the snow line for the first time. Then it drops, steeply, into the Sutlej valley, and you lose about 1,800 metres of altitude in a couple of hours. This descent is disorienting: you leave Shimla going up, and arrive at the river going sharply down, and it will not feel like you are on a road into high Tibet at all. You aren’t yet.

Kotgarh, and the man who gave Himachal its economy

Just off the road near Narkanda is Kotgarh, and the story here is one of the strangest in modern Indian history.

In 1904, a 22-year-old American named Samuel Evans Stokes Jr. arrived in India. He came from a wealthy Philadelphia Quaker family — his father had made a fortune manufacturing elevators — and he had come, over his parents’ objections, to work at a leprosy home at Subathu in the Shimla hills.

He did that for two years and grew disillusioned with organised mission work. He lived for a time as something close to a hermit. He went to Kangra after the 1905 earthquake and helped with relief. He married a local Christian woman, Agnes Benjamin, and settled at Kotgarh. In 1932 he formally converted to Hinduism and took the name Satyanand.

In between, he did the thing he is remembered for. In the mid-1910s, having concluded that the climate and soil of the Shimla hills were right for it, he imported apple saplings from the Stark Brothers nursery in the United States — the Delicious variety — and planted them at Barobagh near Kotgarh. He had gone to America with soil samples first, to check. The trees began bearing within about a decade. And then, crucially, he did not keep it to himself. He gave saplings and knowledge to his neighbours and pushed them to plant.

The result is the landscape you are driving through. Apples are now the backbone of the Himachal hill economy, grown across most of the state’s districts, and the industry traces to that one orchard. Through Kinnaur you will see apple crates stacked by the roadside in September — this is peak harvest — and trucks running them down to the plains. That entire economy is roughly a century old and began with an American who couldn’t settle on a religion.

There is more to Stokes than apples. He joined the Indian National Congress, attended the Nagpur session in 1920, and was jailed by the British for his political activity — the only American to serve a prison sentence in the cause of Indian independence. Gandhi knew him and praised him. He built a temple, Paramjyoti, on his estate. His descendants still live at Kotgarh.

Kipling had been in this valley earlier, and set his short story “Lispeth” at Kotgarh, about a hill girl raised by missionaries and destroyed by a casual English lie.

Rampur Bushahr

Down at the river is Rampur, which was the winter capital of the kingdom of Bushahr — the state whose territory you will be inside for the next three days.

Rampur’s importance was the Lavi fair, held in November, one of the great trans-Himalayan trade fairs. Traders came down from Tibet, Ladakh, Kinnaur and Spiti with wool, pashmina, horses, dried fruit, borax and salt, and went back with grain, cloth and manufactured goods. The fair still runs. It is now mostly a horse-and-handicraft affair, but for centuries it was the point at which the economy of the Tibetan plateau was converted into the economy of the Indian plains.

You will notice, driving through, that the Sutlej here is not beautiful. It is being worked hard. Nathpa Jhakri and Karcham Wangtoo are among the largest hydroelectric projects in India, and much of the river’s water spends much of its journey inside tunnels bored through the mountain. What you see in the riverbed is often what is left over. This is one of the genuine arguments of modern Kinnaur — the projects brought roads, money and jobs, and they are also blamed locally for slope instability, dust, cracked houses and the drying of springs. You will hear both versions if you ask.

Hatu Peak, and a temple worth the detour

Above Narkanda a rough road climbs to Hatu Peak (about 3,400 m), where there is a small wooden temple to Hatu Mata, identified locally with Mandodari, Ravana’s wife. It is a short detour and gives you the first proper Himalayan panorama of the trip. If the morning is clear and you are early, it is worth the hour.

Near the temple is a flat rock formation known as Bhim Chulha — “Bhima’s hearth” — said to be where the Pandavas cooked during their exile. You will meet the Pandavas again at Kamru, at Chandratal and at Manali. They are the connective tissue of the entire route’s mythology.

Nirath, and a sun temple nobody visits

Between Narkanda and Rampur, at Nirath on the bank of the Sutlej, there is a stone temple to Surya, the sun — probably ninth or tenth century, in the stone shikhara idiom of the plains rather than the wooden pagoda idiom of the hills.

That is what makes it interesting. It is one of very few surviving sun temples in North India, and it sits in a valley whose architecture is otherwise entirely timber. Somebody, a thousand years ago, built a plains temple here in plains style, which tells you that the connection down the Sutlej to the Gangetic world was live and strong long before Dalhousie’s road.

It is right beside the highway and almost nobody stops.

Chapter 3

Sarahan

Night 1 · 16 September · 2,165 m

The summer capital of a mountain kingdom

Sarahan sits on a shelf above the Sutlej gorge with the Srikhand Mahadev massif filling the sky to the north. It was the summer capital of Bushahr, with Rampur down at the river as the winter capital, and the reason it matters is the temple in the middle of it.

Bhimakali

The Bhimakali temple is one of the finest wooden buildings in the Himalaya and among the most important religious sites in Himachal. It is built in kath-kuni style, a construction method you will see all the way through Kinnaur and should learn to recognise: alternating horizontal courses of dressed stone and deodar beams, dry-laid, no mortar, with the timber acting as a tension layer running through the masonry.

This is not decoration. It is earthquake engineering, arrived at empirically over centuries. Rigid masonry cracks and collapses in a quake; the timber courses let the wall flex and absorb energy. In the 1905 Kangra earthquake, which killed tens of thousands, Bhimakali’s main tower tilted — and then, the story goes, straightened itself in a later tremor. Whether or not that particular detail is true, the building is still standing, which is the relevant fact.

The complex has twin towers, one older than the other, gold- and silver-plated doors, and a multi-tiered slate roof. Bhimakali is the kuldevi, the clan goddess, of the Bushahr royal family, and the temple is counted among the fifty-one Shakta pithas. The current form owes much to reconstruction under Raja Padam Singh in the early twentieth century.

Look closely inside and you will find something that is easy to miss and tells you everything about where you are: Hindu and Buddhist images in the same building. Bronzes of both traditions, decoration drawing on both. Sarahan is the last place going up the valley where Hinduism is unambiguously dominant, and the Buddhist objects here are the first hint of the world you will be in by Day 4. The temple sits directly on the old Indo-Tibetan road, and trade brought the images.

The other story

Whose head is under the gate

There is a raised platform in front of the entrance, before the first courtyard. Local tradition says a head is buried under it.

The story goes like this. This place was Shonitpur, and its king was Banasura, a demon king — great-grandson of Prahlad, son of Bali, and a devotee of Shiva. Banasura had a daughter, Usha. Usha fell in love with Aniruddha, grandson of Krishna, and the affair brought Krishna’s army to Shonitpur. Shiva, bound by his devotee’s claim on him, fought on Banasura’s side against Krishna.

The versions diverge on the ending. In one, Banasura is beheaded, and his head is what lies under the platform. In another, kinder version, Vishnu had promised Prahlad that his line would be spared, so Krishna stops short of killing Banasura and merely severs a thousand of his arms.

Either way, afterwards Pradyumna — Krishna’s son — took the throne, and the Bushahr dynasty traces itself to him. A line of a hundred and ten kings is claimed between Pradyumna and Raja Chatter Singh, who is said to have shifted the capital from Kamru in the Baspa valley down to Sarahan around the tenth century.

A second story attaches to the same site. When Sati immolated herself at her father Daksha’s sacrifice, Shiva carried her body across the world in grief, and Vishnu cut it apart to end his madness. Wherever a piece fell became a pitha, a seat of the goddess. At Sarahan, they say, her ear fell. Hence Bhimakali, hence the pilgrims.

There is a darker tradition too, which the temple does not advertise and which nineteenth-century British accounts recorded with predictable relish: that human sacrifice was practised here until it was suppressed in the colonial period. The evidence is thin and mostly hearsay, and it is the sort of claim colonial administrators were primed to find. Treat it as folklore about folklore.

What Sarahan is actually like

Mild. Green. Apple orchards, deodar, rain quite possible in mid-September. You will feel you have over-packed. Hold that thought for six days.

The pheasantry, and a very odd bird

Beside the temple complex is a small government pheasant breeding centre, and it is worth ten minutes.

The reason is the Western tragopan — Tragopan melanocephalus, the state bird of Himachal Pradesh, locally called jujurana, “king of birds”. The male is black, spotted white, with a blood-red neck and, in display, two blue fleshy horns and an inflatable blue-and-red bib that it unfurls like a piece of Renaissance tailoring. It is one of the most spectacular birds in Asia and one of the most endangered, confined to a narrow band of the western Himalaya, with a substantial share of the surviving population in the forests around here and in the Great Himalayan National Park.

Sarahan’s centre is one of the few places on earth where they have been bred in captivity. Seeing one in the wild is a serious ornithological achievement. Seeing one here takes five minutes and costs almost nothing.

What the Bushahr kings actually did

It is easy to say “the kingdom of Bushahr” and leave it as a word. Some texture.

Bushahr was one of the larger of the Simla Hill States, a cluster of around thirty small principalities in these mountains — Bushahr, Keonthal, Jubbal, Bhagal, Dhami, Kunihar, Balsan, Theog and the rest — most of them tiny, some of them a single valley, all of them nominally sovereign and practically dependent.

Bushahr’s peculiarity was reach. By controlling the Sutlej valley up to the Tibetan frontier it controlled the trade, and its involvement in the 1679–84 war got it upper Kinnaur. That gave it a length of about 200 kilometres of mountain and a border with Tibet, which mattered enormously to the British after 1815, when they displaced Gurkha power in these hills and began dealing with the hill rajas directly.

The state ran on land revenue, transit dues on trade, and begar. Its capital moved from Kamru to Sarahan and then to Rampur as the centre of gravity shifted from the high valleys down to the river and the fair. It kept a court, a wazir, a small force, and a very strong relationship with its clan goddess.

The Raja was, in a real sense, the goddess’s officer. Bhimakali’s sanction underwrote the dynasty’s legitimacy, and the relationship between temple and throne here was not decorative. When the state merged into India in 1948, the family continued; Virbhadra Singh, son of the Raja Padam Singh who rebuilt this temple, went on to be chief minister of Himachal Pradesh six times, and was known throughout his political life as Raja Sahab.

Which means the dynasty that traces itself to Krishna’s son, that ruled from a fort at Kamru, that fought a Tibetan general in 1679, ended up running the state government until well within living memory. That is an unusually smooth transition from divine kingship to electoral politics.

Interlude Two

How to Read a Kinnauri Village

Useful from Day 1 evening at Sarahan through Day 4.

Once you know what you are looking at, a Kinnauri village stops being picturesque and becomes legible. Here is the grammar.

The buildings: kath-kuni

The technique is called kath-kuni — from kath (wood) and kuni (corner) — and once you see it you will see nothing else all the way to Nako.

How it works. Alternating horizontal courses: a layer of dressed stone, then two parallel deodar beams laid along the wall with a gap between them, then stone filling the gap and forming the next course, then two more beams. The beams are joined at the corners with interlocking dovetail joints. There is no mortar anywhere. The whole wall is held by gravity, friction and joinery.

Why it works. In an earthquake, a mortared masonry wall is brittle: it cracks and then it falls. A kath-kuni wall is a composite. The timber courses take tension, which stone cannot; the mass of the stone gives it damping; and because the joints are dry, the whole structure can flex and slide a little and absorb energy instead of shattering. It is, in engineering terms, a base-isolated confined-masonry system arrived at without a single equation.

The proof is standing in front of you. The 1905 Kangra earthquake — magnitude around 7.8, tens of thousands dead — did comparatively little damage to kath-kuni buildings across the hills, including Bhimakali at Sarahan, whose tower tilted and stayed up. Modern concrete buildings in the same valleys have since performed considerably worse.

What to look for:

  • The banding. Stone-wood-stone-wood, like a layer cake, is the signature.
  • Dovetailed corners. The cross-lapped beam ends projecting at the corners.
  • Slate roofs on heavy timber, steeply pitched to shed snow.
  • Carved balconies and window screens, often extraordinary, on the upper floors, which were the living floors; the ground floor was for animals, whose body heat rose.
  • Storage above, grain and fodder in the loft.

Kath-kuni is in decline for two reasons: deodar is protected and expensive, and concrete is faster and reads as modern. Conservation architects have been trying for years to make the case that the old system is better suited to a seismic zone than the new one. The government has begun to listen; the market has not.

The temples

Kinnauri and Himachali temples are wooden pagodas, not the stone shikhara temples of the plains. Tiered roofs, deep eaves, carved wooden facades, a courtyard, and often a pole or a metal standard.

The reasons are practical and cultural at once: timber, snow load, seismicity, and a tradition of woodcarving that ran continuously for a thousand years. What is unusual, and worth noticing at Bhimakali and again at Hidimba in Manali on Day 9, is that the same architectural language covers deities from very different traditions. This is a regional style, not a sectarian one.

The clothes

Not a costume. Daily wear, still, especially among older people and always at festivals.

The cap. The Kinnauri topi is the emblem of the district — a flat-topped round woollen cap with a broad band of coloured velvet folded up around it. The band’s colour carries meaning locally, and it is worth knowing that the green-banded cap is generally the Kinnauri one and the maroon-banded cap is the Bushahri or Shimla-hills style, though this has become politically inflected in Himachal and people will tell you different things.

The shawl. Kinnauri shawls and pattus are woven in geometric bands of colour and are among the best textiles in the Himalaya. A real one is expensive because it took a very long time.

The rest. Chamu kurti — a woollen shirt. Chamu sutan — long woollen trousers, worn by men and women. Gachhang — a cloth tied at the waist as a belt. Dohru — a heavy woollen wrap worn by women, pinned at the shoulders. Silver jewellery, substantial and often old.

You will also see the Tibetan chuba in the upper valleys, which is the point at which you know you have crossed over.

What grows here

Apples, obviously. September is peak harvest and you will spend four days sharing the road with apple trucks. Kinnauri apples fetch a premium; the high, dry, sunny conditions produce a better fruit than the Shimla belt.

Chilgoza, which almost nobody outside the region knows about and which is arguably more interesting. The chilgoza pine, Pinus gerardiana — named for the Gerard brothers — produces an edible pine nut, known locally as neoza or ri. Kinnaur holds essentially the only chilgoza-producing forests in India; the species otherwise occurs in Afghanistan, Pakistan and a sliver of Kashmir. It grows on steep rocky slopes between about 1,800 and 2,600 metres, it is slow-growing, and it cannot really be farmed.

The harvest, in September and October, is communal and hair-raising. Men climb very tall pines and knock down the cones. The whole village then sits on rooftops splitting cones with small axes and picking the nuts out by hand. In some villages the cones are pooled and divided equally among households, regardless of age, sex or caste — and some cones are always left on the tree for the birds, which are the trees’ seed dispersers.

The nuts went down to the Lavi fair at Rampur for centuries. They are now worth well over a thousand rupees a kilo, and the forests are under pressure from over-harvesting, immature collection and a changing climate.

At weddings, garlands of chilgoza are given to the hosts.

Also: apricots, walnuts, buckwheat, barley, rajma, and at Ribba, grapes — which are turned into a local wine and, more famously, into angoori and arak, the Kinnauri spirits. Kinnaur has a drinking culture that would surprise anyone who assumes rural India is dry.

Water: the kuhl

In the plains, water arrives. In the mountains, water must be led.

A kuhl (also kul) is a contour channel, often kilometres long, that takes water from a snow-fed stream and walks it along the hillside at a very shallow gradient to the fields. Building one is a major work. Maintaining one requires an entire institution: rules about who gets water when, a kohli or water-master, collective labour to clear it each spring, and a mechanism for resolving disputes — which was, of course, often the village deity.

Kuhls are the least photographed and most important structures on this route. Every terrace you admire exists because someone talked water across a mountainside. And they are the point at which climate change bites hardest: a kuhl fed by a glacier that is retreating, or by snow that now falls as rain and runs off in a week instead of melting through the summer, stops delivering at the time of year the crop needs it.

The festivals

If your dates were different you might catch:

  • Fulaich (also Phulaich), the flower festival, late summer — villagers climb to high meadows for flowers, which are offered and worn, with feasting, archery and the remembrance of the dead.
  • Sazo / Sazi, around September — the deities are said to withdraw to the heavens for the winter, and are ritually seen off.
  • Beesh / Bishu, mid-April — the deities come out of their temples.
  • Dakhrain, October, and the Kinnaur Tribal Festival at Reckong Peo in late October and early November.
  • Lavi at Rampur, from 11 November, three days, three hundred years old.

The kayang is the characteristic Kinnauri group dance: a long linked line or circle, arms interlocked, moving slowly and deliberately, sometimes for hours. It looks simple. It is not.

Sidebar

What You Will Hear

Read anywhere. Useful before any festival or morning prayer.

The soundtrack of this road is more specific than you might expect.

In Kinnaur. The characteristic ensemble is loud and processional: the karnal (a long straight trumpet), the ransingha (a curved S-shaped horn), shehnai-type reeds, and dhol and nagara drums. When a deity’s palanquin moves, this is what moves with it. The sound is designed to be heard across a valley.

The kayang is the group dance — a long linked line or circle, arms interlocked at the back, moving in a slow deliberate step, sometimes for hours, sometimes all night. Variants have names and there are regional differences from valley to valley. The associated songs are call-and-response and can run to great length.

Remember from Interlude One that the instrumentalists at these events have historically come from the Scheduled Caste artisan communities. The deity cannot travel without the music, and the musicians could not enter the temple.

In Spiti. A different world. Monastic sound: long dungchen (telescopic horns that produce a low, felt rather than heard, rumble), gyaling (double-reed shawms with a piercing tone), rolmo (heavy cymbals), nga (frame drums), kangling and the dril-bu hand bell. Chanting is deep, slow, in unison, occasionally in the extraordinary low overtone style that Gelug chantmasters use.

If you can be at Ki for morning prayers, you will hear the full ensemble, and it is the single best hour on the itinerary.

The buchen of Pin have their own repertoire — narration, song, drum and cymbal, and the painted scroll unrolled beside them.

And in Lahaul and Kullu, on the last day, you come back into the world of the deity ensembles, at their densest anywhere.

One request. If you record or film a ritual, ask first, and do not put a lens between yourself and a ceremony for its whole duration. The music is not being performed for you. That is exactly what makes it worth hearing.

Chapter 4

The Baspa Valley — Sangla, Kamru, Rakcham, Chitkul

Night 2 · 17 September · Chitkul, 3,450 m

The turn off the highway

At Karcham, the Baspa river comes in from the east to meet the Sutlej, and you turn off NH-5 and climb into a side valley that is one of the most beautiful in the Himalaya and also a dead end. The Baspa rises on the Tibetan watershed; the road stops at Chitkul; beyond Chitkul is a footpath, the Lamkhaga pass, and then Garhwal.

This is where Kinnaur starts feeling like Kinnaur.

Who the Kinnauras are

The name is the first clue. In Sanskrit literature the Kinnaras are a class of celestial beings — musicians, half-human, associated with the mountains, attendants of Kubera the god of wealth. Kalidasa mentions them. They are not quite gods and not quite people, which is exactly how the plains imagined whoever lived up here.

The people themselves are a composite. Historians identify layers: Kiratas, the old Himalayan peoples of Sanskrit sources; Khasas; Indo-Aryan arrivals; and, in the upper valleys near the Tibetan border, Bhotia populations speaking Tibetan-related languages. Kinnaur today has several distinct languages, including Kinnauri, Bhoti Kinnauri, and — in Chitkul alone — Chitkuli Kinnauri, its own language, spoken by a few thousand people and by nobody else.

What makes Kinnaur religiously unusual is not that Hinduism and Buddhism coexist. It is that they coexist within the same household. You will see a Buddhist chorten in the compound of a Hindu temple at Kamru. Families will observe both. Asking someone whether they are Hindu or Buddhist can produce genuine puzzlement, because in the valley the question has not historically been forced.

Layered under both is the deota system: every village has its own local god, with a physical palanquin, a set of oracles and attendants, and real authority. Village deities settle disputes, approve marriages, veto projects and are carried to each other’s festivals to confer. When you see a decorated palanquin being carried by men in a jostling rhythm, you are watching a god travel.

On polyandry, carefully

Kinnaur is known outside the region for fraternal polyandry: a woman married to a set of brothers. It was widespread, it is now uncommon and declining, and it was not exotic — it was land policy. In a valley where cultivable terraces are scarce and inheritance splits holdings, marrying brothers to one wife keeps the family land in one piece across generations. Tibet, Ladakh and Spiti did the same for the same reason.

The local explanation, offered warmly and often, is Draupadi and the Pandavas. The Pandavas are said to have spent part of their exile in Kinnaur, and the custom is traced to them. It is a better story than land fragmentation. Both are true in their own register.

Kamru

Two kilometres above Sangla, at about 2,900 m, sits Kamru and its tower fort — seven storeys, the lower two of stone, the upper five of timber, built in the same kath-kuni technique as Bhimakali but stacked vertically like a keep.

Kamru was the original capital of Bushahr, before Sarahan, before Rampur. This is where the dynasty begins. Whatever you make of the Pradyumna story, the fort is the root of the kingdom you have been driving through since Narkanda. Inside there is a shrine to Kamakhya Devi, whose image is said to have been brought here from Assam, which is a very long way for a goddess to travel and a useful reminder that these valleys were never as isolated as they look.

Chitkul

The last village. 3,450 m. Beyond it the valley narrows towards the Tibet watershed and the road ends.

Chitkul is where the trip stops being a hill holiday. The temperature drops hard, the wind comes down the valley, the buildings change to low stone and timber with heavy roofs, and the night — 0 to 4 °C — is a rehearsal for everything after Tabo. It is also, in September, extremely beautiful: the Baspa braided across a wide gravel bed, the last barley or buckwheat being cut, the peaks close enough to feel.

Two practical notes that belong in a history book because they are history. First, this was a trade and grazing route into Tibet, and the border closure after 1962 killed it. The valley’s outward orientation was north and east for a thousand years; now it is south and west, down the highway. Second, the village has an old wooden temple to Mathi Devi, the local goddess, and she, not the Indian state, is who most people here would tell you actually runs the valley.

The Baspa, and what a side valley is for

The Baspa is a classic hanging tributary: it joins the Sutlej at Karcham considerably above the Sutlej’s own bed, which is why the road has to climb so sharply from the junction. The main river has cut down faster than its tributaries could keep up.

What that produces is a valley with a wide, flat, gravelly floor — braided channels, meadows, and space for fields and villages — sitting above a gorge. This is why the Baspa is green and open when the Sutlej valley two hundred metres below is a slot. Every good village site in the Himalaya is a piece of flat ground that a river left behind, and the Baspa has more of them than most.

Sangla, Rakcham, Batseri

Sangla is the main village, with a bazaar, and is notably prosperous. The valley grows apples, and — unusually — saffron and rajma and the best of the region’s stone fruit.

Batseri, between Sangla and Rakcham, has a fine village temple to Badri Narayan and a magnificent old kath-kuni granary complex. Village granaries are worth noticing: a communal or clan grain store, raised on stone, tightly joined, mouse-proof, cold. In a valley with one harvest and a long winter, the granary was the most important building after the temple.

Rakcham sits where the Baspa braids across a wide flat at about 3,100 m, and is the loveliest village in the valley. The name is said to come from rak (rock) and cham (bridge).

Chitkul: what “the last village” actually means

Chitkul is routinely described as the last village before the Tibet border, and that is true, but the interesting version is what being last meant.

The Baspa leads to the Lamkhaga pass (about 5,280 m), which crosses to Harsil in Garhwal, and beyond the head of the valley lies the Tibetan watershed. Chitkul was a stage on routes that went both east into Tibet and south-east into Garhwal. It was a grazing and trading village on a through-route, not a dead end.

Then the border closed. Now the road ends here because the state ends here, and the traffic is tourists.

Two more things. Chitkuli Kinnauri, the local language, is distinct enough from other Kinnauri varieties to be classed separately, spoken by a few thousand people, and unwritten. It is the kind of language that disappears quietly within two generations of a road arriving.

And the village goddess, Mathi Devi, has a wooden temple with three shrines said to be several hundred years old, with a tradition that the image was brought here from Garhwal. Mathi’s authority in the valley is real and current, and her festival calendar is what the village year actually runs on.

The Kinnaur Kailash parikrama, which you are not doing

A note, because you will hear about it. The Kinner Kailash parikrama is a circuit of the massif of several days, usually done in August, crossing the Charang–Ghati pass at about 5,240 m. It is a serious high-altitude undertaking and people die on it most years. Separately, there is a shorter, brutal pilgrimage direct to the shivling from Tangling.

Neither is a walk. Mentioning it here only so that when somebody in Kalpa says “you can go up to the shivling”, you understand what is being described.

Chapter 5

Kalpa, and the Mountain That Is a God

Night 3 · 18 September · Kalpa, 2,960 m

The view

Kalpa is a village of apple orchards and slate roofs on a shelf above the Sutlej, directly opposite the Kinner Kailash range. In the morning, if the weather is with you, you will step outside and be confronted by a wall of rock and ice about 6,000 metres high, three or four kilometres away, filling the entire eastern sky. Very few places in the Himalaya give you a mountain of that scale that close, at a village you can drive to.

This was Chini, the name under which it appears in nineteenth-century accounts. Lord Dalhousie came here in person — his journey up the road he had commissioned was partly an inspection and partly a test of whether the thing worked.

The Shivling

On the Kinner Kailash massif there is a rock pillar, roughly 24 metres high, standing free on a shoulder of the mountain. It is regarded as a swayambhu Shivling, self-manifested, and it is the object of one of the harder pilgrimages in the Indian Himalaya — the Kinner Kailash parikrama, a circuit of several days at high altitude, undertaken in August.

The pillar is said to change colour through the day, and it does, though the mechanism is the sun rather than the god.

The other story

Whose Kailash is this

This is one of five Kailashes in the Himalayan religious geography, the others being the great Kailash in Tibet, Adi Kailash, Manimahesh and Shrikhand Mahadev. The tradition holds that Shiva winters here — that Kinner Kailash is his residence, and that the assembly of gods and goddesses meets on the massif in winter to deliberate. The Kinnaras, the celestial musicians the district is named for, are his attendants.

Set that against what you will see on Day 4. By then you will be in a landscape where the mountains are inhabited by an entirely different order of beings, and where the local deity of a pass is a Buddhist protector rather than Shiva. You are, over the next thirty-six hours, driving across the edge of one religious universe and into another. The physical border is a stretch of road near Nako. There is no sign.

Reckong Peo, and practical Kinnaur

Below Kalpa is Reckong Peo, the district headquarters — the last place for a good while with functioning banks, a real market, and reliable fuel. The standard advice is that ATMs past Peo are unreliable and often empty in Kaza. Take this seriously. It is not a rural inconvenience, it is a structural fact of the valley economy.

Peo is also the administrative memory of the Inner Line. For decades after 1962, foreigners needed permits to go beyond a certain point in Kinnaur, and Indians faced restrictions too. The line has moved and relaxed, but you are, from here on, in a frontier district with a border a few dozen kilometres away, and the army and ITBP presence you will notice from Pooh onwards is not decorative.

Interlude Three

Who Came Through Here

Read on the Kalpa–Tabo leg, which is where most of them passed.

For about a century and a half, this corridor was one of the most interesting roads in the world to a certain kind of European, and a steady procession of surveyors, naturalists, spies, scholars and obsessives went up it. Several of them stayed in villages you will drive through. A few died doing it.

Here they are, roughly in order.

William Moorcroft (1767–1825)

The founder of the whole genre, and one of the strangest men in the history of exploration.

Moorcroft was a veterinary surgeon — the first Englishman to be formally trained as one — who came to India in 1808 to run the East India Company’s stud and supply the cavalry with horses. He concluded that the horses he needed were in Central Asia, and spent the rest of his life travelling towards them.

In 1812 he crossed the Himalaya into western Tibet, disguised as a Hindu pilgrim, and reached Lake Manasarovar, becoming very nearly the first European to see it. He was arrested on the way back. He then spent years pushing deeper: through Ladakh in 1820–22, where he passed through Lahaul and noted which villages were still paying revenue to Ladakh; into Kashmir; and eventually into Afghanistan and Bukhara, where he died in 1825 in circumstances that have never been satisfactorily explained, and about which there are persistent rumours that he did not die at all.

Moorcroft is the man who first told the British, urgently and repeatedly, that Russia was moving into Central Asia and that the Himalayan passes were a strategic problem. Nobody in Calcutta wanted to hear it. Within twenty years everyone was acting on it. The Great Game, for practical purposes, starts with a vet looking for horses.

The Gerard brothers (1810s–1830s)

Alexander, James Gilbert and Patrick Gerard, Scottish officers in Company service, are the men who mapped Kinnaur. Alexander walked to Shipki La and measured the Sutlej gorge, producing the first reliable altitudes for this region. They were tireless, exact, and interested in everything.

Their surveys are the reason Dalhousie in 1850 knew there was a route worth building a road along. Everything in Chapter 1 rests on them.

Sándor Csoma de Kőrös at Kanam (1827–1830)

This one is worth slowing down for, because it happened in a village you drive past.

Sándor Csoma de Kőrös was a Transylvanian Hungarian, born in 1784, who became convinced that the origins of the Magyar people lay somewhere in Central Asia, and decided to go and find them. He set out in 1819 and walked, largely on foot, across Anatolia, Persia and Afghanistan, into India.

He never found the Magyars. What he found instead was Tibetan.

He lived in monasteries in Zanskar — Zangla from 1823 to 1824, Phuktal from 1825 to 1826 — in conditions that appalled everyone who visited him: a small unheated cell, a sheepskin, and books. Then, from 1827 to 1830, he lived at Kanam in upper Kinnaur, on a British stipend of fifty rupees a month, working through the manuscripts he had gathered, with a Tibetan teacher, the lama Sangye Phuntsok.

What he produced at Kanam was the first Tibetan–English dictionary and the first Tibetan grammar in a European language, published in Calcutta in 1834. He read both of the great canonical collections, the hundred-volume Kangyur and the two-hundred-and-twenty-four-volume Tengyur. He essentially invented the academic discipline of Tibetology, alone, in a village in Kinnaur, in his forties, with no institution behind him.

The Tibetans called him Phyi-glin-gi-grwa-pa, the foreign pupil. In 1933 the Japanese declared him a bodhisattva.

He ended as librarian of the Asiatic Society in Calcutta, living with extraordinary austerity — no drink, no tobacco, almost no company — and died in 1842 at Darjeeling, still trying to get to Central Asia.

At Kanam he was visited by J. G. Gerard and by the French naturalist Victor Jacquemont, who travelled Kinnaur in 1830 and left sharp, funny, slightly cruel letters about everyone he met. For a season or two, in a village on this road, there were three of the more remarkable minds in Asia arguing with each other.

Kanam is between Pooh and the Sutlej, off the highway. There is very little to see. Think about it anyway when you pass.

The boundary commissioners (1846–1848)

After the First Anglo-Sikh War and the Treaty of Amritsar, somebody had to decide where the new border actually ran. The commission sent to do it included Alexander Cunningham, Henry Strachey and the botanist Thomas Thomson, and they went through Spiti and Ladakh doing the work.

Cunningham’s book Ladák (1854) is a foundational text. Cunningham himself went on to found the Archaeological Survey of India and is, more than anyone, the reason we know what we know about ancient Indian sites. He started here, on this frontier, drawing a line.

The Chinese authorities declined to meet the commission. The border consequently never got fully agreed, and the vagueness has been a live problem ever since.

The Schlagintweit brothers (1854–1857)

Three German brothers — Hermann, Adolf and Robert — sent to survey the Himalaya for the East India Company at the suggestion of Alexander von Humboldt. They crossed Kunzum La and worked across Spiti, Ladakh and the Karakoram, producing an enormous body of magnetic, altitudinal and meteorological data and some of the earliest scientific descriptions of the region.

Adolf was beheaded at Kashgar in 1857 by a local ruler who took him for a Chinese spy. He was thirty-eight.

Ferdinand Stoliczka (1838–1874)

A Czech-Moravian palaeontologist in the Geological Survey of India, and one of the finest field scientists of the century. He published on the fossils of Spiti in 1866, which is why Langza is on the scientific map at all.

He died in 1874 at high altitude in Ladakh on the return from the Forsyth mission to Yarkand, almost certainly of what we would now diagnose as altitude sickness, at thirty-six. Read Chapter 11 with him in mind. Henry Hayden continued the geological work here in 1904.

The pundits

Running through all of this, mostly uncredited, are the pundits — Indian surveyors trained by the Survey of India to map Tibet in disguise, because Europeans could not go and Indians could.

They travelled as pilgrims and traders. They were trained to walk a pace of exactly known length and to count paces on a Buddhist rosary modified to a hundred beads. Their prayer wheels held rolled paper notes instead of scrolls. They carried concealed compasses and boiled water to measure altitude by its boiling point. Nain Singh Rawat walked from Kathmandu to Lhasa and fixed its position; Kishen Singh was gone for four years.

They did some of the best survey work of the nineteenth century, at enormous personal risk, and for most of a century the credit went to the men in Dehradun who received their notebooks.

Giuseppe Tucci (1894–1984)

The Italian Tibetologist whose expeditions to western Tibet in the 1930s documented the monasteries of Guge, Tholing and Tsaparang — the parent institutions of Tabo — before the Cultural Revolution damaged or destroyed much of what was there. A great deal of what is known about the world that produced Tabo survives because Tucci photographed it in time. His politics were compromised; his scholarship was not.

George Roerich, and the buchen

George (Yuri) Roerich, son of the painter Nicholas Roerich, was a serious Tibetologist, and in 1932 he published a detailed description of the buchen Ceremony of Breaking the Stone in Pin Valley. His account, along with photographs taken by John Coldstream around the 1910s, is why we know the tradition from outside at all.

The Roerichs settled at Naggar, twenty kilometres south of Manali in the Kullu valley, in 1928. Nicholas Roerich — Russian painter, set designer for Stravinsky’s Rite of Spring, mystic, and the man behind an international treaty for the protection of cultural monuments — painted hundreds of Himalayan canvases from that house. The estate is now a museum. If you have a spare half-day at the end of the trip, go. It is the best art gallery on the entire route and almost nobody on the Spiti circuit knows it is there.

Rahul Sankrityayan (1893–1963)

The greatest Indian traveller of the twentieth century and the necessary corrective to this entire list. Born a Brahmin in Uttar Pradesh, he became successively an Arya Samaji, a Buddhist monk in Sri Lanka, a Marxist, and a member of the Communist Party; learned Sanskrit, Pali, Tibetan, Russian, Persian, Arabic, Chinese and more; made four journeys into Tibet, partly in disguise, and brought back hundreds of manuscripts and thangkas that are now in Patna; wrote well over a hundred books; and was jailed by the British more than once.

His Kinnar Desh is the classic account of Kinnaur — written from inside the Indian tradition, in Hindi, by a man who was interested in the people rather than in the strategic value of the passes.

Heinrich Harrer and Peter Aufschnaiter (1951)

Two Austrians who escaped a British internment camp at Dehradun in 1944, crossed into Tibet, and spent years in Lhasa. Seven Years in Tibet ends with them leaving; Harrer came out over Shipki La, into the valley you drive on Day 4.

Harish Kapadia (b. 1945)

And finally the man who has actually walked most of what you are driving past. Mumbai businessman, mountaineer, editor of the Himalayan Journal for decades, author of Spiti: Adventures in the Trans-Himalaya, and the person who has done more than anyone to document these valleys from an Indian perspective.

He has a useful habit, visible in his review of one European travel book about Spiti, of pointing out that the region is not undiscovered, was never undiscovered, and has been full of teachers, soldiers, scholars, traders and trekkers the entire time — and that the “forgotten valley cut off from the world” framing tells you about the writer, not the place.

Worth remembering when you are writing your own captions.

Chapter 6

The Road Itself

Day 4 · 19 September · Kalpa → Tabo, the long day

This chapter is about the drive rather than a place, because on this stretch the road is the subject.

The gorge

Between Reckong Peo and Khab, the Sutlej is in one of the deepest gorges on earth. The river has cut down through the rising Himalaya for tens of millions of years, and the result is a slot with walls thousands of metres high, in rock that is crushed, sheared and predisposed to move.

The road is cut into the wall of this slot. In places it is a gallery blasted into overhanging rock, in places it runs through unlined tunnels, in places it is a ledge with a drop on one side. Kipling described this road in “The Miracle of Purun Bhagat” as a ten-foot track blasted out of solid rock and strutted out on timbers over enormous gulfs, dipping into warm enclosed valleys and climbing bare grassy shoulders where the sun burns like a lens. The timbers are gone. The description is otherwise current.

Nigulsari and Chaura are the chronic problem areas. Nigulsari failed catastrophically in August 2021, taking a bus with it and killing more than two dozen people. There is no alternate route into Kinnaur. If NH-5 closes here, the district closes.

This is worth knowing not to alarm you but to explain the behaviour you will see: why traffic is stopped and released in convoys, why drivers do not linger in the marked stretches, why everyone accelerates through the shooting zones. Local practice is sound practice. Follow it.

Wangtu, Karcham, and the dams

At Wangtu the road crosses the river and a check post records vehicles. At Karcham the Baspa comes in and the Karcham Wangtoo project takes the water.

The trade-off in Kinnaur is genuinely hard, and worth thinking through rather than sloganeering about. Hydropower here is clean, the state needs revenue, and the projects paid for roads, schools and employment in villages that had none. They have also been associated with slope destabilisation from tunnelling and blasting, with cracked houses, with the drying of springs that villages depended on, and with a river reduced for long stretches to a dry bed with a trickle. Hillsides that were already marginal have been made less stable at the exact moment the monsoon is getting more intense.

There is no clean answer here. There is a real argument, and the people having it live in the villages you are driving through.

Pooh, Khab, and the confluence

Past Pooh — where Rinchen Zangpo, who you will meet properly at Tabo, is said to have founded a monastery a thousand years ago — the road climbs to Khab, where the Spiti river comes in from the north to join the Sutlej.

Stop here if you can. You can see the two rivers before they mix: the Sutlej coming from Tibet, the Spiti coming down from Kaza, different colours, meeting in a V below the road. This is the hinge of your entire trip. Everything before Khab was the Sutlej. Everything after is the Spiti.

A few kilometres up the Sutlej from here is Shipki La, the pass to Tibet, where Dalhousie’s road was actually heading. A nineteenth-century British guide describes Shipki, the village on the far side, in the flat language of a supply list: a large village, where milk, flour, pumpkins and sometimes sheep could be had, with plenty of wood and water. That is what the empire’s road-builders wanted to know about Tibet.

The pass was a major trade route for centuries. It closed in 1962. Limited border trade reopened in 1994 and has been suspended repeatedly since. Heinrich Harrer and Peter Aufschnaiter crossed into India this way at the end of Seven Years in Tibet.

The Ka loops and the change

Above Khab the road does a series of tight hairpins — the Ka loops — to gain height fast, and then you are on the Spiti side. Between here and Nako the world changes.

The trees stop. The green stops. The air goes dry in a way you will feel in your throat within twenty minutes. The rock becomes the raw Tethyan sequence, banded and legible. You have crossed out of monsoon India.

Nako

Nako sits at about 3,660 m around a small lake, with willow and poplar at the water and mud-brick houses stacked behind. It is the first properly Tibetan-looking village of the trip and one of the most atmospheric.

The lake is sacred. Nobody fishes in it, bathes in it, or puts their feet in it. The reason is Padmasambhava — Guru Rinpoche, the eighth-century tantric master credited with establishing Buddhism in Tibet — who is said to have meditated here. In a small white building at the southwest corner of the lake, beneath his statue, there is a rock bearing what is held to be his footprint. A few miles away at Tashigang are caves where he is said to have taught, and where people will show you marks left by other, less identifiable beings.

Nako also has an eleventh-century temple complex with early murals, part of the same Rinchen Zangpo-era building programme as Tabo. If you have time and it is open, go in. The paintings are of the same world as Tabo’s, several centuries deep, and much less visited.

Sumdo, and the detour to the mummy

At Sumdo you formally enter Spiti and there is a check post. This is also the turn-off, about 8 km up a side road, for Gue (also spelt Giu), and if you can spare the hour it is worth it.

In 1975 an earthquake cracked open a tomb at Gue. Inside was a seated human body, skin intact, teeth visible, hair still on the skull. It is now in a glass case in a small shrine, and it belongs to a monk called Sangha Tenzin, carbon-dated to roughly 1475 and estimated to have been in his mid-forties at death. No embalming chemicals have been found. The high nitrogen levels in the body indicate prolonged starvation, and the posture — seated, bound — matches the known practice of self-mummification, in which a practitioner starves the body of fat and fluid over years, then dies in meditation.

It is the only known self-mummified monk in India, and the practice is otherwise associated mainly with Japan.

The local account is not medical. Gue, they say, was suffering a plague of scorpions that was killing people. Sangha Tenzin told his followers to let him mummify himself, as an offering for the village. When he died, a rainbow appeared and the scorpions went.

Interlude Four

The Frontier, 1850 to Now

Read on the Kinnaur leg, or at the Sumdo check post.

The Hindustan–Tibet Road was not built for scenery, and Kinnaur’s unusually heavy military presence is not new. This road has been a strategic object for a hundred and seventy-five years, and it is worth understanding why.

The Great Game

From the 1830s, British India became convinced that Russia was advancing through Central Asia towards the passes of the Himalaya and the Karakoram, and that the buffer states — Afghanistan, Tibet, the Central Asian khanates — would determine whether India was invadable. The resulting contest of surveying, mapping, bribery, disguise and occasional invasion is what Kipling called the Great Game in Kim, and what Russians called the Tournament of Shadows.

Tibet was the great blank on the map. Lhasa was closed to Europeans. So the British tried everything: Moorcroft in disguise, the pundits with their counted paces and hollow prayer wheels, expeditions from Sikkim, and eventually, in 1904, Younghusband’s invasion, which shot its way to Lhasa, imposed a treaty, and achieved almost nothing except to convince the Chinese that Tibet needed re-asserting.

Kinnaur mattered in this because Shipki La is one of the most direct routes from the Indian plains to western Tibet. Dalhousie’s road was pointed at it. The Gerards surveyed it. Trade ran over it. And the British wanted to know what lay beyond.

The line that was never drawn

Here is the structural problem that has never gone away.

The functional boundary between Tibet and the Indian hill states was the one established after the 1684 Treaty of Tingmosgang — in the Kinnaur sector, essentially the Shipki pass. It was a customary border, agreed between the parties who lived there, understood by traders, and never surveyed or demarcated on the ground.

When the British sent the boundary commission in 1846–48, the Chinese authorities declined to participate. So the line was never jointly fixed. Various British maps drew it various ways. At independence in 1947, India inherited a frontier that was clear in practice and vague on paper.

In 1950 China took control of Tibet. The buffer vanished. India now had a direct land border with a major power, defined by custom rather than treaty, in terrain nobody had mapped properly.

1962

The war of October–November 1962 was fought mainly in Ladakh and in what is now Arunachal Pradesh, not here. But its consequences landed on this road with full force.

The border closed. Shipki La shut, and with it a trade that was older than most European nation-states. Hereditary trading partnerships between families in Kinnaur and families in Tibet ended within a season. The Ladarcha fair lost its Tibetan traders and moved to Kaza.

The Inner Line came down. Kinnaur and Spiti became restricted frontier districts. Foreigners needed permits. Movement was controlled. Spiti, which had always been remote, became closed. It did not open properly to outside visitors until 1992, which is why the tourism story here is only about thirty years old and why there is a generation of Spitians who remember when strangers simply did not come.

The road was rebuilt for war. The importance of NH-5 was re-established in blood and money. Major construction followed through the 1960s, and the memorial at Jeori to the 122 men who died building the road belongs to this era as much as to Dalhousie’s.

And Tibetans came the other way. Refugees crossed after 1959. The Tibetan government-in-exile settled at Dharamshala, a few hundred kilometres west. Spiti’s monasteries, previously peripheral to a religious world centred on Lhasa, found themselves part of an exile Buddhism whose institutional centre had relocated to India. The Dalai Lama’s Kalachakra at Ki in 2000 belongs to that history.

Now

The border is quiet here and has been for decades, but it is not settled. Limited trade at Shipki La reopened in 1994 and has been repeatedly suspended. The 2020 Ladakh standoff at Galwan re-militarised the whole Himalayan frontier and accelerated Indian border infrastructure — which is part of why the Atal Tunnel’s strategic value features so heavily in the way it is described.

The ITBP posts, the army convoys, the signs telling you not to photograph installations, the check posts at Wangtu and Sumdo and Losar: all of that is 1684, 1846, 1904, 1950, 1962 and 2020, still running.

Be polite at check posts, carry your ID, and don’t photograph military installations. The rule is not decorative.

Interlude Five

What Moved Over the Passes

The economic history in one page. Read at Khab or Kaza.

For roughly a thousand years, this corridor was a functioning trade route, and understanding what actually moved on it explains almost every settlement on your itinerary.

Going south, out of Tibet:

  • Pashmina — the undercoat of the Changthangi goat, the most valuable commodity in the Himalaya, the raw material of the Kashmir shawl industry. Whole wars were fought over control of this trade; Zorawar Singh’s invasion of Tibet in 1841 was substantially about it.
  • Wool — coarser sheep and yak wool in bulk.
  • Borax — mined from Tibetan lake beds, used as a flux in metalworking and in glass. A major export for centuries.
  • Salt — from the salt lakes of the Changthang, and for inland Himalayan communities this was the essential trade: salt from the plateau, grain from the valleys.
  • Gold dust, musk pods, semi-precious stones, yak tails, livestock — horses and yaks.

Going north, into Tibet:

  • Grain — barley, wheat, rice from lower down. The fundamental exchange of the Himalaya is always grain-for-salt.
  • Sugar, cotton cloth, spices, metalware, dyes, and later manufactured goods.
  • Tea, in compressed bricks, though the main Tibetan tea route ran from China rather than India.
  • Chilgoza, dried apricots and walnuts from Kinnaur.

The mechanism. Much of this moved through hereditary trading partnerships, household to household across the border, sustained over generations. A family in Kinnaur or Spiti had a counterpart family in Tibet; you dealt with them, extended them credit, housed them, and your children dealt with their children. It was not an anonymous market. It was a web of personal obligations that happened to move goods.

The fairs. Trade concentrated at seasonal fairs. Lavi at Rampur in November, three hundred years old, brought the Tibetan and Ladakhi trade down to the Sutlej. Ladarcha, originally held on the maidan at Kibber in July and later moved to Kaza in August, brought traders from Ladakh, Rampur Bushahr and Spiti together to barter. Both fairs still run. Ladarcha moved to Kaza and became a cultural festival after the Tibetan traders stopped coming.

And then it stopped. The 1962 war closed the border. Shipki La shut. Hereditary partnerships that had run for generations ended in a single season. Families that had wintered on cross-border trade had to find something else.

What replaced it was the road. Everything now comes up from the plains and goes down to the plains, and the valleys that had faced north and east for a thousand years turned around to face south and west. Kinnaur got apples. Spiti eventually got peas and tourists.

The Lavi fair still sells Kinnauri woollens, chilgoza, apricots, horses and metalware. It is now a domestic fair with an international memory.

Interlude Six

How to Read a Monastery

Read this the night before Tabo. It will change what you see for the next four days.

You are about to walk into four or five very dark rooms whose walls are entirely covered in figures, and unless someone tells you what the figures are, they will resolve into “beautiful old paintings” and slide off your memory within a week. This chapter is the key.

First: the orders, in ninety seconds

Tibetan Buddhism is not one thing. It has schools, they have histories, and the monasteries on your route belong to different ones. This matters because the art, the emphasis and the politics differ.

Nyingma — “the ancient ones”. The oldest school, tracing to Padmasambhava in the eighth century. Less centralised, more tantric, married lamas common, strong emphasis on treasure-texts (terma) hidden by Padmasambhava and rediscovered later. On your route: Nako’s associations, Kungri in Pin Valley, and the buchen tradition.

Kadam — the reform lineage founded by Atisha and his disciple Dromtön in the eleventh century, austere and text-focused. It no longer exists independently; it was absorbed into the Gelug. On your route: the original foundation at Ki.

Sakya — founded in the eleventh century, rose to rule Tibet in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries under Mongol patronage. This is the period that destroyed Ki’s predecessor. On your route: Tangyud at Komic, and the Sakya gompa at Kaza.

Kagyu — the lineage of Marpa, Milarepa and Gampopa, meditation-centred, the source of the Karmapas. Dominant in Ladakh and Bhutan. Less present in Spiti but the treaty of 1684 was signed by a Drukpa Kagyu hierarch.

Gelug — “the virtuous ones”, the Yellow Hats, founded by Tsongkhapa around 1400, emphasising monastic discipline and rigorous philosophical training. The school of the Dalai Lamas. It became politically dominant in Tibet in the seventeenth century under the Fifth Dalai Lama, with Mongol military backing — which is precisely why Ki was plundered by Mongols and came out the other side a Gelug institution. On your route: Tabo, Ki, Dhankar, Lhalung — most of Spiti.

So the violence in Chapter 10 is not random banditry. It is the Tibetan sectarian-political struggle reaching into a poor border valley and rearranging its monasteries.

The building, from outside in

Chortens (stupas). The whitewashed monuments with a square base, a dome, a spire of rings and a sun-and-moon finial. They are reliquaries and symbols: the base is earth, the dome water, the spire fire, the parasol air, the finial space. Some hold relics, some hold texts, some commemorate a lama. Always pass them with the chorten on your right — walk clockwise.

Mani walls. Long, low walls faced with flat stones carved with mantras, usually om mani padme hum. Each stone was carved and placed by someone as an act of merit. Some walls have been growing for centuries. Pass on the left of the wall so the wall is on your right.

The mantra itself. Om mani padme hum is the mantra of Avalokiteshvara, the bodhisattva of compassion, of whom the Dalai Lama is held to be an emanation. Literally it is something like “the jewel in the lotus”, but Tibetans generally treat the meaning as carried by the sound rather than by translation.

Prayer wheels. Cylinders packed with printed mantras, turned clockwise. Turning one is held to release the mantras. There are hand-held ones, rows set into monastery walls, and big ones turned by water or by a whole room’s worth of visitors.

Prayer flags. Five colours in a fixed order: blue (space), white (air/wind), red (fire), green (water), yellow (earth). They are printed with mantras and with the wind horse (lungta), and the theory is that the wind carries the prayer, not that the flag delivers a wish. They are meant to fade. A bleached, tattered flag has done its job. Do not take one, and do not burn or bin one.

The roof. Look for the dharma wheel flanked by two deer — the Buddha’s first sermon in the deer park at Sarnath. Also victory banners (cylindrical cloth standards) and often a set of yak-tail or juniper offerings.

Entering: the etiquette that matters

  • Shoes off. Always, in the shrine rooms.
  • Clockwise. Around the room, around the central image, around the building, around the chorten, around everything. If in doubt, keep the sacred thing on your right.
  • Hats off. Sunglasses off.
  • Do not point your feet at an image, an altar, a text or a lama. If you sit, sit cross-legged or kneel, or tuck your feet under you.
  • Do not point with a single finger at an image. Use an open hand.
  • Do not touch the paintings. The pigments at Tabo are a thousand years old and skin oil is corrosive.
  • Photography: assume no, in old shrine rooms, unless explicitly told otherwise. Flash is actively destructive. Ask.
  • Do not step over texts, ritual objects or people’s legs.
  • Do not walk in front of someone prostrating or praying.
  • Leave a donation, in the box. The upkeep of a thousand-year-old mud building is not free, and the entry fees do not cover it.
  • Morning prayers are generally open to quiet visitors. Sit at the back, on the floor, and stay still. It is the best hour of the day at Ki.

Who is on the wall

Here is the cast. You will meet most of them repeatedly.

Shakyamuni Buddha — the historical Buddha. Usually seated cross-legged, right hand reaching down to touch the earth (the earth-witness gesture, at the moment of enlightenment), left hand in his lap with a begging bowl. Often blue-black haired with a cranial bump. Gold or yellow body.

Maitreya — the Buddha of the future, waiting in the Tushita heaven for his turn. Often shown seated in a chair with both feet on the ground, which is unusual and a giveaway. Frequently the largest image in a Spiti temple.

Avalokiteshvara (Tib. Chenrezig) — compassion. Often white, four-armed, two hands at the heart holding a jewel. Sometimes with eleven heads and a thousand arms, each palm bearing an eye: the form he took after his head split from grief at the suffering he could not relieve.

Manjushri (Tib. Jampelyang) — wisdom. Orange-yellow, holding a flaming sword in his right hand (cutting through ignorance) and a lotus supporting a book in his left.

Vajrapani (Tib. Chana Dorje) — power. Dark blue, wrathful, holding a vajra (thunderbolt sceptre). Avalokiteshvara, Manjushri and Vajrapani together are the “three family protectors” and you will see the trio everywhere.

Tara — the female bodhisattva of swift compassion. Green Tara (action, protection from fear) and White Tara (healing, long life) are the common forms. Seated with one leg extended, as if about to stand up and help.

Vairochana — one of the five “cosmic” Buddhas, white, hands in the teaching gesture at the chest. Vairochana is central to the Rinchen Zangpo-era temples: at Tabo, Nako, Alchi and Lhalung, the eleventh-century programme is often built around him. If you see a white four-faced Buddha at the centre of the composition, you are looking at the signature of this period.

Padmasambhava (Guru Rinpoche) — the eighth-century tantric master. Recognisable by a moustache, a slightly fierce staring expression, a lotus hat with a feather, a vajra in his right hand, a skull-cup in his left, and a staff (khatvanga) in the crook of his left arm. Dominant in Nyingma settings: Nako, Kungri.

Tsongkhapa — founder of the Gelug school. A monk, yellow pointed hat, hands in the teaching gesture, flanked by a sword and a book on lotuses at his shoulders. Usually with his two chief disciples. If you see this trio, you are in a Gelug building.

The wrathful protectors (dharmapalas) — terrifying figures with bulging eyes, fangs, skull crowns, flames, sometimes trampling bodies, sometimes in sexual union with a consort. These are not demons. They are enlightened beings in wrathful form, and the imagery is deliberate: the flames are wisdom burning ignorance, the skulls are the ego’s defeat, the union is the fusion of compassion and wisdom. They are usually in a separate, darker room (the gonkhang), sometimes veiled, sometimes closed to women by local custom.

The Four Guardian Kings — painted on either side of the entrance porch of most gompas, one for each direction. White with a lute (east), blue with a sword (south), red with a stupa or mongoose (west), yellow with a banner and mongoose (north). They guard the doorway. Walk between them.

The one image to actually stop and study: the Wheel of Life

On the porch wall of most monasteries, usually to the right of the door, is a large circular diagram held in the jaws and claws of a monster. This is the bhavachakra, the Wheel of Life, and it is the entire Buddhist analysis of existence in one picture. If you learn one thing to look at, learn this.

The monster holding the wheel is Yama, impermanence or death. Everything inside the circle is inside his grip. That is the point.

The hub — three animals chasing each other in a circle, each biting the next’s tail: a pig (ignorance), a snake (hatred), and a cock or bird (desire). These are the three poisons that keep the whole wheel turning.

The next ring — a band split light and dark, with figures ascending on one side and tumbling down on the other: beings rising to better rebirths and falling to worse ones.

The six segments — the six realms of rebirth:

  1. Gods — luxurious, blissful, and doomed, because pleasure ends and they have made no progress.
  2. Demi-gods (asuras) — jealous, fighting the gods over a wish-fulfilling tree whose roots are in their realm and whose fruit is in the gods’.
  3. Humans — the only realm with enough suffering to motivate and enough comfort to allow practice. This is the good one. This is you.
  4. Animals — driven by fear and appetite, unable to reflect.
  5. Hungry ghosts (pretas) — enormous bellies, pinhole mouths, needle throats. Endless craving, no possible satisfaction. The most psychologically modern image in the whole system.
  6. Hells — hot and cold, with elaborate torments, all of them temporary.

Look closely and you will find a small Buddha figure in each of the six realms. The teaching is that there is a way out from everywhere.

The outer rim — twelve small scenes, the twelve links of dependent origination, the causal chain by which the whole thing perpetuates. A blind woman with a stick (ignorance), a potter (formation), a monkey (consciousness), a boat with passengers, a house with windows, a couple embracing, an eye pierced by an arrow, someone drinking, a man picking fruit, a couple coupling, a woman giving birth, a corpse carried to a cremation.

Outside the wheel, in the top corner, the Buddha points at the moon, or at a text. That is the exit.

Sit with it for ten minutes at Tabo or Ki. It is one of the great pieces of visual philosophy anywhere, and it is painted on the outside wall where anyone illiterate could learn the whole system by looking.

The other things in the room

Thangkas — painted scroll paintings on cloth with brocade borders, hung or rolled. Portable, used in teaching and in the buchen’s storytelling.

Butter lamps — brass bowls of clarified butter with a wick. The smell of a Tibetan shrine room is rancid butter, juniper smoke and old wood, and you will remember it long after you have forgotten the paintings.

The texts — long, loose-leaf, unbound, wrapped in cloth and stacked between wooden boards in pigeonholes along the walls. This is the Tibetan book form, copied from Indian palm-leaf manuscripts. Tabo’s and Ki’s collections include genuinely rare material. Never step over one or put one on the floor.

Kangyur and Tengyur — the two great canonical collections: the Kangyur is the word of the Buddha in translation (about a hundred volumes), the Tengyur is the Indian commentarial literature (over two hundred). Csoma read both, in a cell, in Kinnaur.

Tsampa and butter tea — if they are offered to you, take them. Butter tea (po cha) is salty, greasy and, at 4,000 metres in a cold room, exactly correct. Think of it as soup and you will get on with it much better.

Chapter 7

Tabo

Night 4 · 19 September · 3,280 m

A mud building from the year 996

Tabo village is a low scatter of houses on a flat terrace beside the Spiti river, with the cliff behind it pocked by meditation caves. In the middle of it is a walled compound of mud-brick structures that look, from outside, like almost nothing. No gilded roof. No tiered pagoda. Just rammed earth and whitewash, weathered, patched, entirely unspectacular.

It is the oldest continuously functioning Buddhist monastery in India and the Himalaya, and the paintings inside are among the great treasures of Asian art.

Tabo was founded in 996 CE, in the Tibetan year of the Fire Ape. It has never closed. It has been in use for a thousand and thirty years.

Why it is here: the story of Yeshe-Ö

To understand Tabo you need a piece of Tibetan history that is not widely known in India, and it starts with a collapse.

The Tibetan Empire — the one that in the seventh to ninth centuries was a genuine great power, occupied the Chinese capital, controlled the Silk Road oases and fought the Arabs and the Chinese to a standstill — fell apart in the 840s. Central authority disintegrated. The monasteries were abandoned. Buddhism in Tibet very nearly ceased to exist.

A descendant of the old royal line fled west and established a kingdom in Guge, in far western Tibet, in the high desert near Mount Kailash. His grandson took monastic vows and is known to history as Yeshe-Ö, roughly 947 to 1024. Yeshe-Ö decided to bring Buddhism back, properly, with correct texts and correct doctrine, and he went about it like a state project.

In 971 he selected twenty-one young men and sent them south across the passes to Kashmir — then one of the great centres of Buddhist scholarship — to learn Sanskrit, study, and bring the teachings back.

Two came home. The heat of the Indian plains, disease and the journey killed the rest.

One of the two survivors was Rinchen Zangpo (958–1055), and he is the reason this road has the buildings it has. He returned to Guge in 988 and spent the next six decades translating Sanskrit Buddhist texts into Tibetan on a scale that reshaped the religion. He is known simply as Lochen, the Great Translator. He brought Kashmiri artists back with him — tradition says thirty-two of them — and he built.

Legend credits Rinchen Zangpo with 108 temples across the western Himalaya. The real number is unknown and smaller, but the surviving list is extraordinary: Tabo and Lhalung in Spiti, temples at Nako and Pooh in Kinnaur, Alchi, Lamayuru, Mangyu and Sumda Chung in Ladakh, Tholing in Guge. A single generation of building whose survivors are still, a thousand years later, the most important early Buddhist art in the Himalaya.

Tabo, founded in 996 on Yeshe-Ö’s behalf, is the best preserved of all of them.

What to look at

The compound holds nine temples, four decorated stupas and more than twenty chortens, over roughly 6,300 square metres. The heart of it is the Tsuglakhang, the main temple, built in 996 and substantially renovated in 1042.

Some orientation for when you are inside:

  • The entrance chamber holds images of Yeshe-Ö himself with his two sons. The founder is physically present at the door of his own foundation.
  • The assembly hall opens to a shrine with a circumambulation passage. Walk clockwise.
  • The walls are covered, floor to ceiling, in painting and stucco sculpture. The figures project from the wall in relief, life-size, ringing the room.
  • Many of the painted portraits are named in inscriptions — royal patrons, local nobility, monks. This is exceptionally rare. You are not looking at generic donors. You are looking at identified eleventh-century people from this valley.

It is called the Ajanta of the Himalaya, and the comparison is fair in one specific way: both are places where the entire interior surface is a painted programme, and both are rare survivals of a style otherwise lost.

Two practical things. The interiors are unlit — no electric light is permitted near the paintings, and for good reason. Bring the headlamp from your packing list and use it sparingly and low. Photography is not allowed inside the old temples. Accept this. The pigments are a thousand years old and flash is not neutral.

The other story

What Yeshe-Ö did at the end

The famous version of Yeshe-Ö’s death is this. He was captured by a Qarluq Turk king, who offered to ransom him for his weight in gold. His people gathered the gold. Yeshe-Ö, from prison, told them not to pay — to use the gold instead to invite the great Indian master Atisha to Tibet, because a teacher was worth more to Tibet than an old king. He died in captivity.

Atisha did come to Tibet, in 1042, and the reform he brought is one of the hinges of Tibetan religious history. Rinchen Zangpo, by then in his eighties, became his student.

The scholar David Snellgrove argued the prison story is legend, pointing out that Yeshe-Ö issued a royal edict in 1027 and that Rinchen Zangpo’s own biography says the king died of illness in his palace at Tholing, with Rinchen Zangpo performing the funeral rites.

Keep both. One is what happened. The other is what the tradition decided the man meant.

Chapter 8

Dhankar and Lalung

Day 5 · 20 September · Tabo → Mud, via the Dhankar turn-off

The fort on the cliff

Dhankar is a name made of two words: dhang, cliff, and khar, fort. Fort on a cliff. It is exactly that.

The old gompa sits on a spur of crumbling conglomerate about three hundred metres above the confluence of the Spiti and Pin rivers, and the setting is one of the most dramatic of any monastery in the Himalaya — buildings apparently growing out of a knife-edge of rock that looks, correctly, as though it is in the process of falling down.

Dhankar was the capital of Spiti. Before Kaza, this spur was the seat of power, the fort, the treasury and the prison. Parts of the structure go back to the twelfth century; it was the working capital through the seventeenth.

The Nono

The rulers of Spiti were called the Nono. Under whichever larger power claimed the valley — Ladakh usually, Kullu sometimes, Guge before that — the actual day-to-day authority was the Nono’s, and the office was hereditary.

The arrangement was remarkably durable because Spiti was poor. A governor sent from Leh would arrive, collect after the harvest, and go home before winter, leaving the Nono to run everything. When the British took over in 1846, they looked at the place, calculated the revenue, and did exactly the same thing. The Nono of Kyuling, near Kaza, was confirmed as hereditary wazir of Spiti, a position formalised in the Spiti Frontier Regulations of 1883. Total annual revenue extracted from the entire valley by the British in the 1840s was around 700 rupees.

The Nonos dispensed justice from Dhankar and were remembered for harsh penalties. There is a cell in the fort complex that was the jail.

The old gompa is structurally fragile — it has been on the World Monuments Fund watch list — and access is sometimes restricted. A newer monastery sits below at Shichilling. Above the old fort, a short and reasonably steep walk gets you to Dhankar Lake, which at 4,100 m is a hard forty-five minutes if you are not yet acclimatised. Judge it honestly on Day 5.

Lalung

If your route allows the detour, Lalung has the Serkhang, the Golden Temple, another of the Rinchen Zangpo-era foundations, with early sculpture and painting of the same family as Tabo’s. It is less visited and more atmospheric, and the village sits in a side valley of poplar and barley that is startlingly green against the rock.

The geology of a monastery that is falling down

Dhankar’s setting is spectacular precisely because the rock is bad.

The spur it stands on is a conglomerate — an ancient cemented mass of gravel, glacial and river debris, laid down when the valley floor was much higher. It erodes into vertical pinnacles and fluted columns because water cuts down through it fast but it stands up in cliffs while it does so. The whole spectacular skyline of eroded towers around Dhankar is that process in action.

Which is also why the monastery is in trouble. The material it is standing on and partly built from is unconsolidated and moving. Dhankar has appeared on the World Monuments Fund watch list, there has been conservation work, and access to parts of the old complex is sometimes closed. If it is shut when you arrive, that is why.

What a Spiti fort was for

Dhankar answers a question you might not have thought to ask: why does a valley this poor need a fort?

Because it sat on a route. Spiti is a natural corridor between Ladakh, Tibet, Kinnaur and Kullu, crossed by passes in every direction — Kunzum to Lahaul, Pin–Parvati to Kullu, Pin–Bhaba to Kinnaur, and the Parang La north to Ladakh. Anyone moving between those worlds came through here. That made Spiti worth taxing, worth raiding and worth controlling, and it is why the list of armies in Chapter 10 is so long.

A fort at the confluence of the Spiti and the Pin controls both valleys. Put the treasury, the jail and the ruler in it, and you have a state.

Lhalung, and the Serkhang

If the detour is possible, Lhalung (about 3,700 m) is one of the quiet highlights of Spiti.

The Serkhang, the Golden Temple, is another of the foundations attributed to the Rinchen Zangpo period. What makes it remarkable is the sculpture: figures in stucco projecting from the walls in high relief, ringing the room, of a quality and antiquity that would make them a national treasure anywhere with better transport links.

The village name means “land of the gods”. There is a sacred juniper grove and a tradition that the trees change colour to warn of misfortune. Photography inside is not permitted.

Chapter 9

Pin Valley

Night 5 · 20 September · Mud, 3,800 m

A different valley

The Pin comes into the Spiti at Dhankar from the south, and the valley behind it is separate in more than geography. It is greener in patches, narrower, and religiously distinct: while most of Spiti is Gelugpa — the Yellow Hat order of the Dalai Lamas — Pin is a stronghold of the Nyingma, the oldest of the Tibetan Buddhist schools, which traces itself to Padmasambhava in the eighth century.

Kungri, partway up the valley, is the Nyingma monastery, and the oldest in Pin.

Mud, where you are sleeping, is the last village, at 3,800 m. The road ends. Beyond it the Pin–Parvati pass leads over to Kullu, and the Pin–Bhaba pass to Kinnaur, both on foot.

The Buchen

Here is the thing about Pin that almost nobody who drives through knows.

The buchen are ritual performers found in the Pin valley and nowhere else. The word means something like “great sons”. They are not monks. They sit outside the ordinary monastic clergy, they marry, and they belong to hereditary households. They are lay religious specialists: exorcists, actors, storytellers, musicians.

Their lineage runs back to Thangtong Gyalpo (roughly 1361–1485), one of the great eccentric figures of Tibetan history. He was a yogi, a physician, an iron-chain-suspension-bridge engineer who is credited with building dozens of bridges across Tibetan rivers, and the inventor of Ache Lhamo, Tibetan opera. He is remembered as a “crazy saint” — a nyönpa, a holy madman, whose teaching worked through paradox, excess and laughter rather than scholastic argument.

His disciples in Pin carry two traditions.

The first is a religious theatre. Through the winter, buchen troupes historically toured the villages of Spiti, upper Kinnaur, parts of Ladakh and formerly western Tibet, performing moral and religious folk tales — a localised form of Tibetan opera. The stories illustrate karma and impermanence, and they also provide a licensed space for obscenity, mockery and earthy humour, which is how such traditions usually survive. They tell stories with painted scrolls, unrolling the images as the narration moves.

The second is the Ceremony of Breaking the Stone, an exorcism. A slab of stone, marked with images of demons, is placed on a man’s belly, and smashed. It has been described by outsiders for a century — photographed by John Coldstream around the 1910s, described by the Tibetologist George Roerich in 1932, and studied in detail by the French anthropologist Pascale Dollfus.

The photographer Patrick Sutherland has worked in Spiti since 1993 and with the buchen for years; his book with the Tibetan historian Tashi Tsering, Disciples of a Crazy Saint, is the standard account. There is a story in it worth carrying: Sutherland once gave the buchen prints of his photographs of them, and was told the pictures were so bad that the performers had torn them up and burned them. He went back and negotiated a different way of photographing them — one that matched how they saw themselves, as religious specialists rather than as picturesque subjects. The book is partly about that negotiation.

You are sleeping in the valley this belongs to. The winter performances are the least documented part of the tradition and may be disappearing.

Snow leopards

Pin Valley National Park covers the upper catchment and is one of the core snow leopard habitats in India, along with the Kibber sanctuary you will pass on Day 7. The local name for the animal is jhatpo.

You will not see one. September is the wrong season and a single night is the wrong duration; sightings are a January-to-March proposition with a spotter and a week. But they are there, in real numbers, and so are their prey: ibex (tangrol), blue sheep, and the small hardy Chamurthi horses that Spiti breeds and that the snow leopards occasionally take.

The economics of this have shifted in an interesting way. Snow leopards used to be purely a cost to villagers — livestock losses, no compensation. Community conservation programmes now make the animals worth more alive than dead, through homestay income and insurance schemes. The homestay you are sleeping in is part of that system.

The Pin–Parvati and what lies behind Mud

Standing at Mud, the road ends and a footpath continues. It is worth knowing where it goes, because it explains why this village exists.

Pin–Parvati pass (about 5,300 m) crosses west into the Parvati valley and down to Kullu. Pin–Bhaba pass (about 4,900 m) crosses south-west into the Bhaba valley and down to Kafnu in Kinnaur. Both were working trade and grazing routes, not adventure trekking routes, and both are now popular and serious treks.

So Mud is not the end of anything. It is a road-head at the bottom of two passes, which is a completely different kind of place. For centuries this was where you loaded the animals before crossing, and where you arrived, exhausted, having crossed.

Why Pin is Nyingma

Spiti is overwhelmingly Gelug. Pin is Nyingma, and the reason is a good illustration of how religious geography works in the Himalaya.

The Nyingma is the old school, rooted in Padmasambhava and the first transmission of Buddhism to Tibet in the eighth century, with a strong tradition of non-monastic, married, householder practitioners — ngagpa — and of lay religious specialists. The Gelug arrived later, backed by political power, and became dominant.

Side valleys retain older things. Pin is up a tributary, off the main corridor, harder to reach, less worth conquering. The buchen — lay, hereditary, married, performing outside the monastic system — are exactly the kind of institution that survives in a place the reformers did not bother to reach.

Kungri, the Nyingma gompa partway up the valley, is the oldest monastery in Pin. Its cham dances, performed at festival time, are among the most striking in the region.

The blue sheep, the ibex and the dogs

One more thing worth knowing before you sleep here, because it is the live conservation argument in Spiti.

Numbers of free-ranging and feral dogs have risen sharply in Spiti over the last two decades, fed by tourism waste, army camps and village refuse. They hunt in packs. They take livestock, and they also take blue sheep and ibex calves — which is a direct hit on the snow leopard’s prey base — and they compete with the snow leopard itself. Researchers working in the upper Spiti landscape have documented this as a significant and growing problem.

It is an unglamorous conservation issue with an obvious cause: rubbish. It ties directly to the plea in Chapter 16 about carrying your waste out. Open food waste in Spiti does not attract birds. It feeds dogs, and the dogs eat the ibex.

Chapter 10

Kaza and Ki

Night 6 · 21 September · Kaza, 3,650 m

The town

Kaza is the administrative centre of Spiti: the sub-divisional headquarters, the hospital, the fuel pump, the market, the bus stand, the mobile signal. It is not old and it is not beautiful. It is the place where Spiti’s modern life happens, and after five days of villages it will feel like a city, which says more about the previous five days than about Kaza.

Fuel here. It is the highest retail pump in the world by some claims, and more importantly it is the last one before Manali.

Ki

Nine kilometres north, on a conical hill above the Spiti river at 4,166 m, is Ki Gompa — also spelled Key, Kye, Kee. It is the largest monastery in Spiti and the image most people carry of the valley: whitewashed boxes stacked up a hill in no apparent order, like something grown rather than built.

That shape has a reason, and the reason is violence.

What happened to Ki

The monastery’s own account and the standard histories give this sequence:

  • Eleventh century. Founded, by tradition, by Dromtön, the principal Tibetan disciple of Atisha — the same Atisha that Yeshe-Ö is supposed to have died to bring to Tibet. This may in fact refer to a now-destroyed Kadampa monastery at nearby Rangrik.
  • Fourteenth century. That earlier foundation is destroyed, probably when the Sakya order rose to power in Tibet with Mongol backing.
  • Fifteenth century. Refounded, in the accounts kept at Ki itself, by Sherab Zangpo, a follower of Tsongkhapa, founder of the Gelug school.
  • Mid-seventeenth century. Plundered and badly damaged by Mongol armies during the reign of the Fifth Dalai Lama. It becomes a Gelugpa establishment.
  • Around 1820. Sacked again in the wars between Ladakh and Kullu.
  • 1841. Severely damaged by the Dogra army under Ghulam Khan and Rahim Khan, during Zorawar Singh’s campaign. Later the same year, attacked again by a Sikh army.
  • 1840s. Ravaged by fire.
  • 1975. Badly damaged by the earthquake that shook the whole valley — the same one that cracked open the tomb at Gue.

Each time, the monks rebuilt on top of the ruins rather than starting elsewhere. The stacked, chaotic form is a thousand years of damage and repair, layer on layer. The architectural classification is pasada — multi-storeyed, fortress-monastery. It was built to be defended, and it needed to be.

In 1855 it reportedly housed a hundred monks. In 2000 the Dalai Lama held a Kalachakra ceremony here. It is a working monastery and a training centre, and if you can get there for the early morning prayers, do.

The wars, briefly

The list above needs a frame, because it looks like random misfortune and it isn’t. Spiti spent nine hundred years as a poor buffer between larger powers, and every time the balance shifted, someone came through and took what there was.

The essential sequence:

Tibetan empire, then Guge. Spiti was drawn into the western Tibetan orbit in the tenth century and was part of the Guge kingdom’s world. That is why Tabo exists.

Under Ladakh, mostly. From the twelfth century onward, Spiti was a dependency of Ladakh when Ladakh was strong and effectively autonomous when it was weak, paying tribute variously to Ladakh, Kullu and Chamba.

1679–1684: the Tibet–Ladakh–Mughal war. Tibet invaded Ladakh over tribute and religious control of Ngari. Ladakh appealed to the Mughals in Kashmir and got help. The war ended with the Treaty of Tingmosgang in 1684, which fixed a border that has effectively lasted to the present.

This war is the reason your itinerary looks the way it does. Two of its settlements matter here:

First: the Raja of Bushahr, Kehri Singh, had involved himself on the winning side, and upper Kinnaur was transferred to Bushahr as a reward. The formula recorded later is that territory above the Shipki pass belonged to Tibet and territory below it to Bushahr. That is why the Kinnaur you drove through on Days 1 to 4 was a Hindu kingdom’s territory rather than a Tibetan one.

Second: Spiti came loose. It was nominally Ladakhi and practically free, and Raja Man Singh of Kullu promptly invaded and made it pay tribute. Control drifted back towards Ladakh in the eighteenth century.

1834–1842: Zorawar Singh. A Dogra general in the service of Gulab Singh of Jammu — himself under Ranjit Singh’s Sikh empire — invaded Ladakh in 1834 and conquered it. In 1841 he over-reached and invaded Tibet, aiming at the pashmina trade. He was defeated and killed in the winter, at high altitude, by Tibetan forces. The armies that wrecked Ki in 1841 were the by-product of this campaign.

1846: the British. After the First Anglo-Sikh War and the Treaty of Amritsar, Ladakh went to Gulab Singh and the new state of Jammu and Kashmir, while Lahaul and Spiti were detached and taken under direct British administration. Spiti was then run, with minimal interference, through the Nono, and eventually attached administratively to Kullu.

1960. Lahaul and Spiti became a single district of independent India, which is why two places separated by a 4,590 m pass and a watershed share a hyphen.

1962. The war with China closed the border, ended the trade, and turned a crossroads into a cul-de-sac. The road you are driving was massively rebuilt in the years after 1962 for military reasons, at considerable cost in lives.

Interlude Seven

How a Spiti Village Works

Read at Kaza, or at Mud the night before.

Spiti is one of the hardest inhabited environments in the world outside the polar regions: under 200 mm of precipitation a year, a growing season of four months, winter temperatures down to −30 °C, and half the year cut off. People have lived here continuously for well over a thousand years. Here is how.

The house

A traditional Spiti house is not a smaller, poorer version of a house elsewhere. It is a specific piece of climate engineering.

Materials. Sun-dried mud brick and rammed earth on a stone footing, with a timber frame of poplar and willow — both planted deliberately, in rows, near water, because nothing else grows. Walls are thick. Mud has high thermal mass: it absorbs the day’s sun and gives it back at night, and it insulates far better than concrete.

The roof. Flat, because there is almost no rain to shed and because the roof is working space: this is where you dry dung, stack fodder, spread apricots and peas, sleep in summer and store the winter’s fuel.

The layout. The ground floor is for animals and storage — livestock downstairs generate heat that rises. The first floor is the living floor, with the main rooms on the south side for sun. There is often a summer kitchen and a separate winter room, small, low-ceilinged and easy to heat, where the whole family effectively lives from November to March around a stove.

The chak-sa. This is the part worth telling people about when you get home. The traditional Spitian toilet is a dry composting toilet on the upper floor: a hole in the floor of a small room, with a sealed collection chamber on the ground floor below, accessible only from outside. Ash and earth are added after each use. In the cold, dry air, the contents dehydrate rather than rot. Once or twice a year the chamber is emptied from the outside, and the composted material goes onto the fields.

Think about what that solves. No water is used in a place with almost none. No waste is discharged into a fragile system. Soil fertility is maintained in a desert with no natural nutrient cycling and no chemical fertiliser. It is a closed loop, it has run for a thousand years, and it is being replaced — in homestays and new builds, for tourists — by flush toilets connected to soak pits, in a valley that cannot spare the water and whose groundwater has nowhere to go.

You will probably use a flush toilet on this trip. Notice it.

Fuel. Dried animal dung, mainly, stacked in neat walls and cavities near the kitchen; plus scrub, juniper, and now LPG and kerosene brought in by road. Dung is not a poverty fuel here, it is a designed part of the system, and the decline of livestock is quietly making winter heating harder.

What is replacing it. Concrete, cement block, glass, tin roofs. They go up faster and read as modern and prosperous. They are also far colder in winter, far hotter in summer, structurally worse in an earthquake, and dependent on materials trucked over Kunzum La. The change has happened within thirty years, largely funded by green peas.

The year

April–May. Snow goes. The kuhls are cleared by collective labour — a whole village turning out to dig ice and silt from irrigation channels that may run for miles. Fields are manured with the winter’s accumulated compost and dung. Ploughing begins, historically behind yaks or dzo (yak-cow hybrids), now increasingly behind a tractor.

May–June. Sowing. One crop a year, and this is it. Get it wrong and there is no second chance.

June–August. Growing and irrigating. Water is allocated by turn, by an agreed schedule, with a village water-master and a great deal of negotiation.

June (low villages) to August (high villages). Green pea harvest, the cash crop, picked and trucked out fast to Chandigarh and Delhi.

August–September. Barley and black pea harvest. These are the traditional crops and they come in later, after the pea work is done. Threshing happens on the ul-tak, a flat threshing ground each household keeps near the fields. This is what you will see happening as you drive: people cutting by hand, small stacks, threshing floors in use.

October. Everything is stored, fodder is stacked, fuel is laid in. The last trucks get out before the passes close.

November–March. Winter. The roads close. Historically the valley was entirely sealed; now the Kinnaur road to Shimla stays open most of the time, unreliably, and Kunzum is shut. This is the season of weddings, festivals, visiting, storytelling and religious performance — including the buchen tours of Pin — because it is the only time nobody has work to do. Households live in one heated room. Water freezes in the pipes and is carried from the spring. Children go to school in Kaza or not at all. The sick are a serious problem.

February–March. Losar, the new year, the biggest festival of the year.

The great pea revolution

Here is the single biggest change in Spiti in a thousand years, and it happened in the 1980s.

Traditional Spiti agriculture was barley — hulless and covered — plus black pea, some wheat, mustard and potato. Barley became tsampa, roasted flour, the staple food, and chhang, barley beer, and arak, the distilled spirit. Surplus barley and black pea were bartered with Changthang traders for pashmina and yak wool. It was a subsistence economy with a trade edge, and it had been stable for centuries.

From the mid-1980s, with road access improving, government encouragement and market demand, farmers began planting green peas as a cash crop. Surveys of Spiti villages found green peas going from nothing to about a quarter of cultivated area by the 1990s and nearly half by 2000. Farmers started travelling to Chandigarh to sell.

Cash arrived in a valley that had barely used it.

What it bought: concrete houses, motorbikes, education for children in Kullu and Chandigarh and Shimla, phones, solar panels, medical treatment, and the ability to leave.

What it cost: peas need much more water and much more labour than barley, in a valley where water is the binding constraint. Barley is drought- and disease-resilient and needs almost nothing; peas are neither. Households became exposed to a market hundreds of kilometres away, so a bad price in Delhi in September is now a bad year in Kaza. Some farmers have gone back to black pea partly because it keeps — a perishable cash crop with a single road out is a fragile thing.

Livestock numbers have fallen sharply in the well-connected villages, because pea income beats sheep and goats and because mechanisation replaced draught animals. Less livestock means less manure, which means the traditional fertility cycle is breaking, which means bought fertiliser, which means more cash dependence.

And now apples are arriving in Spiti — which would have been unthinkable a generation ago and is happening because the valley is getting warmer. More on that in Chapter 16.

Food

Tsampa — roasted barley flour, mixed with butter tea into a dough in the bowl with the fingers. The Tibetan staple for a thousand years. Extremely efficient: pre-cooked, needs no fuel, keeps indefinitely, travels.

Po cha / butter tea — tea churned with salt and butter. Calories, salt and fluid in one cup, which is exactly what you need at altitude in the cold.

Thukpa — noodle soup, usually with vegetables and sometimes meat. Thenthuk — the same with hand-torn flat noodles, and the better of the two.

Momos — steamed dumplings. Universal now; still good.

Chhang — barley beer, cloudy, sour, low-strength, ceremonially important. Arak — the distillate, much less gentle.

Seabuckthorn — the orange berry growing in dense thorny thickets along the river beds, called chharma locally. Extraordinarily high in vitamin C, harvested for juice by local cooperatives, and one of the few genuine value-added products the valley produces. Try it.

Chhurpi — hard dried cheese, chewed for hours.

In Kinnaur, on the earlier days, look for siddu (a steamed stuffed bread, a Himachali speciality), chha gosht, rajma, buckwheat pancakes, and the local apricot and grape spirits.

Medicine: the amchi

The traditional medical system here is Sowa Rigpa, the Tibetan science of healing, and its practitioner is an amchi. It is a genuine, codified system with its own canonical texts, pharmacopoeia of Himalayan plants and minerals, and diagnostic methods based on pulse, urine and questioning.

For most of Spiti’s history the amchi was the only medicine. Amchi practice is often hereditary, and the knowledge of where and when to gather particular high-altitude plants is a significant part of it. The government now recognises Sowa Rigpa formally in India, and there are amchi still practising in Spiti alongside the primary health centre at Kaza.

The relevance to you: altitude sickness, in the traditional framework, is understood and treated, and the standard local advice — go slowly, drink, eat, rest, don’t be proud — matches the modern medical advice exactly.

Leaving, and coming back

The biggest social fact in Spiti now is out-migration. There is no college in the valley. Children who continue schooling go to Kullu, Shimla, Chandigarh or Dharamshala, often from a young age, often boarding. Many do not come back permanently. Government jobs, the army, the tourism trade in Manali, and work in the plains all pull people out.

What comes back is money, and expectations, and a generation that speaks Hindi and English more comfortably than their grandparents and may not have the agricultural knowledge their parents had.

This is neither a tragedy nor progress. It is what happens when a road reaches a valley. But it does mean that some of the things in this book — the buchen’s winter tours, the amchi’s plant knowledge, the technique of building a mud house that stays warm at −30 °C — are one or two generations from disappearing, and the people who know them are aware of it.

Chapter 11

The High Villages

Day 6 · 21 September · Langza, Hikkim, Komic

Day 7 · 22 September · Kibber, Chicham

These two days take you to the highest inhabited places you will ever stand in, and four of them have a claim to some kind of world record. Treat the records lightly and the altitude seriously. This is the part of the trip where your body notices.

Langza — the seabed

Langza sits at about 4,400 m on an open shelf, with a large gilded Buddha looking out over the valley and a few dozen houses behind it. It is one of the most photographed places in Spiti and one of the most geologically important places in India.

Under and around the village lie the Spiti Shale and the underlying Tagling limestone, the Jurassic seabed of Chapter 0. They weather out of the soft slopes, and what weathers out are fossils.

The commonest are ammonites — coiled marine molluscs, relatives of the modern nautilus, extinct at the same moment as the dinosaurs 66 million years ago. Some of the Spiti specimens exceed 30 centimetres across. Alongside them: brachiopods, belemnites, bivalves including Inoceramus, oysters, crinoid stems, corals, fragments of marine reptiles, and aptychi, the jaw plates of ammonites.

More than sixty species of Jurassic ammonite have been recorded from Spiti, and the species links run to Europe and to New Zealand — a measure of how genuinely global the Tethys ocean was. Ammonites are prized by geologists because they evolved fast and spread wide, which makes them index fossils: find a particular species and you can date the rock, anywhere in the world, to within a narrow band.

Spiti has been attracting geologists since the 1860s. Ferdinand Stoliczka published on the fossils here in 1866; Henry Hayden in 1904. Stoliczka was a Czech-Moravian palaeontologist in the service of the Geological Survey of India, one of the most gifted field scientists of his generation, and he died in 1874 at high altitude in Ladakh on the way back from the Yarkand mission, probably of what we would now call altitude sickness. He was thirty-six.

Please do not buy a fossil

This matters, and it is the one request in this book.

Children in Langza will offer you ammonites. Shops in Kaza sell them. It is charming and it is a disaster.

Fossils in India are protected under the Antiquities and Art Treasures Act, 1972. In 2024 the Himachal Pradesh High Court directed the authorities to act against fossil smuggling from the region. In 2025 Langza was designated a national geo-heritage site by the Geological Survey of India, adding protections including the possibility of no-mining zones and increased monitoring.

The problem is not sentimental. A fossil is scientifically useful only in context — which layer, which slope, which association. Prised loose and sold, it becomes an ornament and its information is destroyed. And the trade has a scale problem: hundreds of visitors a day, each taking “just one”.

Look at them. Photograph them. Hold one and put it back. If you want to take something home from a seabed at 4,400 metres, take the fact.

Hikkim — the post office

Hikkim, at around 4,400 m, has the highest post office in the world. PIN code 172114.

It opened in 1983. The first postmaster, Rinchen Chhering, held the job from the beginning. The mail is carried to and from Kaza on foot. It is also a savings bank, and this is the part people miss: for the villages up here, the post office is not a novelty for tourists, it is the financial infrastructure. People deposit and withdraw their money in a room at 4,400 metres, and a man walks the paperwork down the mountain.

It closes for the winter, when snow shuts the passes.

Send a postcard. It is the least cynical tourist ritual on this entire route, because the institution is real and the stamp is real and someone actually walks it out.

Komic — the top

Komic, at 4,587 m, is the highest village in the world connected by a motorable road, and there is a sign saying so. Ten or fifteen houses, spread out, and the Tangyud monastery.

Tangyud is interesting because it is Sakya, not Gelug — one of very few surviving Sakya foundations in Spiti, a remnant of the period when the Sakya order, backed by the Mongols, dominated Tibet. It is built like a small fort, with thick blank outer walls, which is what religious buildings looked like in a landscape where raiding was normal. It is said to have been originally at Hikkim and moved here after an earthquake.

Komic is your highest daytime exposure of the trip. It is above Annapurna Base Camp. In winter it goes to −30 °C and the village is cut off completely. In September at noon it will be cold and windy and the sun will be strong enough to burn you in under an hour. Both of those things are true at the same time, which is the defining weather condition of this whole plateau.

Kibber and Chicham

Kibber (about 4,270 m) is a larger village on a limestone shelf, and the edge of the Kibber Wildlife Sanctuary — prime snow leopard country, the place most of the winter snow leopard tourism in India is based.

Below Kibber is a gorge about a thousand feet deep separating it from the village of Chicham. For generations the only connection was a ropeway: a wire across the void and a trolley you hauled yourself across in, in all weathers, with your goods and your animals and your sick relatives. Going round by road meant a detour of roughly 40 km.

The Chicham bridge opened in 2017. It is a suspension bridge at about 4,145 m, claimed as the highest in Asia, and it crosses the gap in a minute.

Two things about standing on it. First, it is extremely exposed to wind. The gorge funnels. Bring the buff and the beanie, not just for the photograph.

Second, look down and then think about the ropeway. It is very easy, on a trip like this, to be nostalgic about the world before the road. The people who lived here are not nostalgic about the ropeway. This bridge is one of the clearest cases anywhere of infrastructure that unambiguously improved lives, and it took until 2017.

On altitude, honestly

Day 6 is the physiological crux. You will have gone from 3,800 m at Mud to 4,587 m at Komic and back down to 3,650 m at Kaza in one day, and the night in Kaza is often the worst sleep of the trip.

What is normal: breathlessness on short uphill walks, a headache that responds to paracetamol and water, a poor and broken first night, vivid dreams, waking with a start because your breathing paused (periodic breathing at altitude is common and harmless), reduced appetite, and needing to urinate more.

What is not normal and means going down: a headache that does not respond to painkillers, vomiting, confusion, difficulty walking in a straight line, or breathlessness at rest. Any of those, at any point, the answer is descend. Kaza has a hospital. Losar and Chandratal do not.

Water is the single most useful intervention. Three to four litres a day, more than feels reasonable. The air is bone-dry and you are losing water with every breath without noticing.

Chapter 12

Losar and Kunzum La

Night 7 · 22 September · Losar, 4,085 m

Day 8 · 23 September · Kunzum La, 4,590 m

The last village

Losar is the final settlement in Spiti before the pass. It sits at 4,085 m at the confluence of the Losar and Peeno streams, and it has a reputation, consistently, as the coldest inhabited place in the valley. The homestays are unheated or nearly so.

The name is the same word as Losar, the Tibetan new year, and the village is where the Spiti valley effectively ends — beyond it there are no more fields, no more poplar, just the climb to the pass.

It will be a hard night: −4 to −8 °C, no heating, thin walls. This is the night the thermals are for. Change into dry ones before bed even if you don’t feel damp. Sweat from the day is what makes you cold at 3 a.m., not the air.

Kunzum La

Kunzum La, at 4,590 m, is the watershed. It separates Spiti from Lahaul and, more fundamentally, the Sutlej basin from the Chenab basin. On one side, water eventually reaches the Arabian Sea via Punjab. On the other side, water eventually reaches the Arabian Sea via a different set of Punjab rivers. It is the hinge of the entire trip.

At the top is a cluster of chortens, a great deal of prayer flag, and a small temple to Kunzum Devi — identified variously as a form of Kali or as the local protector goddess of the pass.

The custom is that you stop. Everyone stops. You pay respects, you ask for safe passage, and traditionally you drive one full circle around the temple before continuing. Truck drivers do it, taxi drivers do it, people who would describe themselves as entirely secular do it. There is a whole anthropology in the fact that the pass has a goddess and the goddess must be circled, and there is also the simpler fact that this pass kills people and has done for centuries.

The wind at Kunzum is constant. The views take in the Chandrabhaga range and, on a clear day, the Bara Shigri glacier. First snow of the season here typically arrives in late September or early October, which puts your crossing right at the edge of the window.

The other story

The pass as a door

It is worth noticing what happens to the religious landscape as you cross.

Behind you, the valley has been Buddhist for a thousand years — Tabo, Ki, Dhankar, Komic, Kungri. Ahead of you, in Lahaul, you are moving back towards a zone where Buddhist and Hindu traditions interpenetrate again, and by Manali you will be in a valley of Hindu village gods, temples to Manu and Hidimba, and the Kullu Dussehra.

But the pass itself belongs to neither exactly. It belongs to the goddess of the pass. Across the Himalaya, from Kunzum to Rohtang to the passes of Ladakh, the deity of the high crossing is older than the religion on either side of it — a local power that must be acknowledged by everyone who crosses, whatever they believe at home. The prayer flags and the chortens on one side and the red cloth and trident on the other are two vocabularies for the same thing.

You will pass through it in ten minutes in a car. For most of human history, passing through it took a day, and you might not make it.

What Losar is actually like to live in

Four thousand and eighty-five metres. About a hundred and fifty people. One crop a year. Snow on the ground for something like five months. The road to Kunzum shut from roughly November to May, and the road down-valley to Kaza unreliable in the same period.

This is the last village in the Spiti valley, and the people here are the ones who have to deal first with anything coming over the pass — which historically meant armies, and now means tourists, and in both cases meant feeding and housing whoever arrives.

Losar is where the Ladarcha trade fair’s traffic once came through, where Spiti’s connection to Lahaul and Kullu was made and broken by the season, and where the phrase “cut off for six months” was not a figure of speech until quite recently.

The two streams that meet here, the Losar and the Peeno, form the Spiti river. You have driven the Spiti from Khab, 200 kilometres downstream, and this is where it begins.

The name

Losar is the Tibetan new year: lo (year) and sar (new). Whether the village name is the same word is disputed, and it is also glossed as something like “the place where you cross”.

Losar as a festival falls in February or in some Spiti traditions earlier, and it is the major event of the year: masked dances, house-cleaning, the setting up of offerings, visiting, feasting, drinking and the ritual disposal of the previous year’s bad luck. Spiti observes a local variant, and the calendar varies between villages — which is itself a good illustration of how much religious authority sits at the village level rather than in any centre.

Practical, because this is the hardest night

  • The homestay is unlikely to be heated. Ask about a bukhari (stove) and about what heating costs, which your operator page already flags.
  • Eat properly. Appetite drops at altitude and you need the calories to stay warm overnight. Have the thukpa even if you do not feel hungry.
  • Fill your thermos before bed.
  • Hot water bottle into the sleeping bag half an hour before you get in.
  • This is the second-highest night of the trip and often the one people find hardest, because Chandratal is anticipated and Losar is not.
Chapter 13

Chandratal

Night 8 · 23 September · 4,337 m · the night everything is packed for

The lake

Chandratal — Chandra Taal, the Moon Lake — sits at about 4,300 m on the Samudra Tapu plateau, in a basin of scree and old moraine, roughly six kilometres from Kunzum La by the road and a short walk from the camps. It is named for its crescent shape. It is the source of the Chandra river.

The water changes colour through the day, from steel to turquoise to a deep green, depending on light and angle and the suspended glacial flour in it.

It is a Ramsar site — a wetland of international importance — and part of the Chandratal Wildlife Sanctuary. This is why the camps are set back and why the rules about the shoreline exist. There are ruddy shelduck on it, and ibex and blue sheep in the surrounding slopes, and snow leopard in the wider sanctuary.

Historically it was a staging point. Traders moving between Spiti and the Kullu valley, and Tibetan traders coming through, camped here. It was a place on a route, not a destination, which is a useful corrective to the way it is photographed now.

The other story

Yudhishthira’s last walk

This is the best story on the whole route and it deserves telling properly.

At the very end of the Mahabharata, in the book called the Mahaprasthanika Parva, the war is long over and the Pandavas are old. Krishna is dead. Their kingdom is settled on a grandson. So the five brothers and Draupadi renounce everything and set out to walk north, into the Himalaya, towards the mountain of the gods, intending to walk until they die.

A dog attaches itself to them on the road and follows.

One by one, they fall. Draupadi first. Then Sahadeva, then Nakula, then Arjuna, then Bhima. Each time a brother falls, Yudhishthira keeps walking without turning, and when Bhima, dying, asks why each of them fell, Yudhishthira names the flaw: Draupadi’s partiality, Nakula’s vanity about his beauty, Arjuna’s pride in his bow, Bhima’s greed. He says it plainly and walks on.

Eventually only Yudhishthira and the dog are left.

Indra descends in his chariot and tells Yudhishthira that he alone may ascend to heaven in his own body — an honour given to no one. Yudhishthira says: then the dog comes too.

Indra refuses. There are no dogs in heaven. The dog is impure. It is unthinkable.

Yudhishthira refuses to go. He will not abandon a creature that took refuge with him and stayed loyal when everyone else fell away. He would rather forfeit heaven. He says so, and holds it, against the king of the gods.

At which point the dog reveals itself as Dharma — righteousness itself, Yudhishthira’s own divine father — who has been testing him the whole way, and who tells him he has passed.

Local tradition places the spot where Indra’s chariot came down at Chandratal.

You are camping there. In the cold, with no electricity, no network, twelve hours of dark, and more stars than you have seen in your life. It is not a bad place to think about a man who declined heaven over a dog.

There are other stories attached to the lake — a love story about Chandra, daughter of the moon, and Bhaga, son of the sun, who meet where the lake is, giving their names to the two rivers that join at Tandi to make the Chandrabhaga. And a persistent modern folklore about lights in the sky, which at 4,337 m with no light pollution is a story that more or less writes itself.

The sky, properly

You have gone to a great deal of trouble to be in one of the darkest places accessible by road in India, at the clearest time of year, at 4,337 metres, with no moon interference in the early part of the night around the 23rd. Here is how to use it.

First, protect your night vision. Full dark adaptation takes about thirty minutes and a single glance at a white phone screen destroys it instantly. Put your phone away. If you need light, use a red torch, or tape red cellophane over a torch, or cup your fingers over the lens so only a dull glow escapes. Tell your camp-mates you are doing this so they don’t shine a headtorch in your face.

Let your eyes learn. The first five minutes are unimpressive. At fifteen minutes the Milky Way becomes obvious. At thirty minutes you will be able to see structure in it — the dark lanes, which are not empty space but interstellar dust clouds blocking the light of stars behind them.

What is up there in late September, after dark:

  • The Milky Way runs from the northeast down to the southwest, high overhead in the early evening. You are looking along the plane of our own galaxy. The bright, complicated part low in the southwest is towards the galactic centre, in Sagittarius, 26,000 light years away and setting.
  • The Summer Triangle is nearly overhead: Vega (in Lyra, brilliant, slightly blue), Deneb (in Cygnus, the top of the Northern Cross), and Altair (in Aquila). Cygnus lies along the Milky Way, and the dark rift splitting the band there is the Great Rift, a chain of dust clouds.
  • The Andromeda Galaxy (M31) is high in the east. Find the Great Square of Pegasus, then step up and left along the chain of Andromeda’s stars. It is a faint elongated smudge, best seen by looking slightly to one side of it (averted vision). It is 2.5 million light years away and it is the most distant object the unaided human eye can see. In a sky this dark it is straightforward.
  • The Double Cluster in Perseus, northeast, a faint patch resolving into two knots.
  • The Pleiades rise in the east later in the evening — a tight, obviously blue knot of stars. Count them; most people see six, keen eyes see more.
  • Cassiopeia, the W, high in the north. The Plough low in the north.
  • Jupiter and Saturn may be visible depending on the year’s arrangement; anything very bright that does not twinkle is a planet.
  • Satellites cross constantly, moving steadily without blinking. Aircraft blink. Starlink trains, if one passes, look like a string of beads and are unmistakable.
  • Meteors: no major shower is active, but the sky is dark enough that sporadic meteors are frequent. Expect several an hour if you are watching.
  • Airglow and zodiacal light: in skies this dark you may see a faint diffuse cone of light along the ecliptic after full dark — sunlight scattered off interplanetary dust. Most people have never seen it.

A thought for the middle of the night. The light from Andromeda that hits your retina left that galaxy two and a half million years ago, when the first members of the genus Homo were walking in Africa. It crossed intergalactic space for all of human existence and arrived at a lake in Spiti on the night you happened to be lying beside it, on rock that was the floor of an ocean.

Chapter 0 and Chapter 13 are the same chapter, looked at in opposite directions.

Practical: watch from the sleeping bag if you can, or wear everything. Twenty minutes standing still at −8 °C in wind is genuinely dangerous if you are underdressed and it will end your evening. Take a flask of something hot. Lie down rather than crane your neck.

What the night is actually like

Be realistic. Sunset to sunrise is about twelve hours. It will be −5 to −10 °C, colder with wind off the glaciers. There is no charging, no hot shower, no network, and the tents are canvas.

Practical things that make the difference:

  • Get in the sleeping bag before you are cold, not after. Once your core drops it takes hours to recover.
  • Change into dry thermals. Again. Same reason.
  • Hot water bottle if you brought one. This is what it was for.
  • Keep the phone and any camera batteries inside the bag with you. Lithium cells lose a large fraction of their capacity below freezing. A phone at 60% left on the tent floor can be dead by morning.
  • Do not dehydrate yourself to avoid getting up at night. Drink. Getting up in the cold is annoying; altitude plus dehydration is worse.
  • Camps are set back from the lake because it is a protected site. The walk is part of it.

By common agreement of everyone who has done this route, this is the best night of the trip. It is also the hardest. Both.

Chapter 14

The Chandra Valley

Day 9 · 24 September · Chandratal → Batal → Gramphu → Manali

The last day drops you about 3,000 metres in one day, from Chandratal to Manali. This is the biggest single change of the trip, and you will feel it as the air thickening, the temperature climbing, and layers coming off one by one from Koksar onwards. Manali will feel tropical. It is at 2,000 m.

Batal

Batal is a wide gravel flat at the foot of the Kunzum descent where the road meets the Chandra river, and it is not a village. It is a couple of structures in an enormous empty valley.

It is also the site of one of the genuinely well-loved institutions of the Himalaya: the Chandra Dhaba, run for decades by Dorje and Chhering Angmo, universally known as Chacha and Chachi. For years theirs was the only shelter on a fifty-kilometre stretch of road at 3,900 m, and they fed and housed whoever turned up — trekkers, truck drivers, stranded travellers, breakdowns. They are locally credited with saving lives during a mass stranding in 2010, when they sheltered and fed a large group of stranded travellers for days. Chacha died a few years ago; the dhaba has continued.

Stop for tea. It is the last hospitality of the cold desert.

Bara Shigri

A few kilometres from Batal, on the far bank of the Chandra, is the snout of the Bara Shigri — “big glacier” in the Lahauli dialect, where shigri means boulder-covered ice.

It is the largest glacier in Himachal Pradesh: about 28 km long and covering roughly 126 square kilometres at the last full survey. It feeds the Chandra, which feeds the Chenab. Most of its lower surface is buried under moraine so thick that you cannot see ice at all for long stretches — what you see is a slow river of rubble, which is what most Himalayan glaciers actually look like at the bottom.

It is retreating, and has been measured doing so since 1906, when H. Walker and E. H. Pascoe surveyed it for the Geological Survey of India and set painted cairns as reference points. When a Geological Survey expedition returned in 1956 for the International Geophysical Year, the cairns had all been destroyed by frost and the comparison had to be rebuilt from scratch. Earlier still, Captain Harcourt described it in 1869.

The Shigri flood

The most spectacular thing the glacier ever did happened in the 1830s. The Shigri surged forward across the valley and dammed the Chandra river with ice and rubble. A lake built up behind the dam.

Then the dam failed.

The flood ran down the Chandra and into the Chenab and did serious damage far down the valley. These events — glacial lake outburst floods, GLOFs in the modern jargon — are among the most dangerous hazards in the Himalaya, and they are getting more frequent as glaciers retreat and leave unstable moraine-dammed lakes behind them. Chungthang in Sikkim in 2023 and Kedarnath in 2013 were versions of the same thing.

You are standing in the valley it ran down.

Chhota Dara, Gramphu, Koksar

The road follows the Chandra down through Chhota Dara — another lonely dhaba stop — with the river on your left and glaciers hanging off the Pir Panjal on your right. The Chhota Shigri, across the way, is one of the most intensively monitored glaciers in India, a benchmark site for Himalayan mass-balance studies, which means a lot of what is known about how fast the Himalaya is losing ice comes from the slope you are driving past.

This stretch of road is usually the worst of the entire trip: unpaved, braided with stream crossings, and slow. It improves at Gramphu, where you join the Manali–Keylong highway.

At Gramphu you have a decision that would not have existed six years ago.

Rohtang, or the tunnel

Rohtang La, at 3,978 m, was for centuries the only way in and out of Lahaul. The name is usually glossed as something like “pile of corpses”, and while the etymology is disputed, the sentiment is not: this is a pass that killed traders and shepherds regularly, and it closed the valley for six months a year. Lahaul spent every winter cut off.

The Atal Tunnel opened on 3 October 2020. It runs 9.02 km under the Pir Panjal at about 3,100 m, from a south portal 25 km from Manali to a north portal near Sissu in Lahaul. It is a single-tube, two-lane, horseshoe-section tunnel with a full-length emergency egress tunnel running beside it, built by the Border Roads Organisation using the New Austrian Tunnelling Method.

It was sanctioned in 2002 and took eighteen years, delayed by water ingress, rock bursts and one notoriously bad fault zone at Seri Nala. It cuts the Manali–Keylong journey from five or six hours to about two, reduces the Manali–Leh distance by 46 km, and gives Lahaul year-round road access for the first time in its history.

What that means, concretely: a person in Lahaul having a medical emergency in January can now reach a hospital. That is the tunnel’s real argument, and it is unanswerable.

What it also means: Lahaul got mass tourism overnight. Sissu went from a quiet village to a day-trip destination for Manali crowds within a season. Land prices, traffic, waste and water have all become issues in a valley that had no infrastructure for any of it. The tunnel is a straightforwardly good thing that has produced complicated effects, which is the general condition of roads in this book.

Coming from Gramphu, the pragmatic choice is usually the tunnel: faster, warmer, and no Rohtang traffic. The Rohtang road, if it is open and you have time and the day is clear, gives you the last big view back over Lahaul and the classic descent into the Kullu valley. Both are defensible. Your drivers will know the conditions.

Either way, watch what happens at Koksar and below: the first green in five days, the first trees, the first real rain shadow crossing. You will start pulling off layers and not stop.

Chapter 15

Manali

Arrival · 24 September · 2,050 m

Coming down

Manali will assault you. After nine days in which the loudest thing was wind, you will arrive into traffic, horns, cafés, paragliders, hotel touts and a large number of people on holiday. This is not a criticism of Manali. It is a description of what a 3,000-metre descent does to your nervous system.

Give yourself an hour before forming an opinion.

Manu

The town is named for Manu, and the claim is large: this is where Manu landed.

In the Hindu flood story, Manu — the progenitor of humanity, the first man — is warned of a coming deluge by a fish, which he has raised from small to enormous and which turns out to be Vishnu in the form of Matsya. Manu builds a boat, loads it with the seeds of all living things and the seven sages, and ties it to the fish’s horn. The flood comes and drowns the world. The fish tows the boat through the waters and finally grounds it on a northern mountain. From Manu, humanity begins again.

The Kullu valley claims this as its own origin, and there is a Manu temple in Old Manali, said to be the only temple to him in India. Manu-alaya, the home of Manu, is the usual etymology offered for the town’s name.

It is a good story to end on. You have spent nine days looking at a landscape that was under an ocean, holding the shells of the animals that lived in it, and you finish in a town whose founding myth is a man in a boat after a flood, waiting for the water to go down.

Hidimba

The Hidimba Devi temple in Old Manali is a four-tiered wooden pagoda built in 1553 by Raja Bahadur Singh, standing in a deodar grove. It is built around a rock, and the object of worship is a footprint on the rock rather than an image.

Hidimba is a rakshasi from the Mahabharata. During the Pandavas’ exile in the forest, her brother Hidimba attacked them; Bhima killed him; Hidimba fell in love with Bhima and married him, and their son Ghatotkacha became one of the great figures of the war, a half-demon warrior who dies heroically on the fourteenth night at Kurukshetra.

In the Kullu valley she is not a monster. She is a goddess, one of the most important in the region, and the Kullu royal family traditionally sought her sanction. There is a separate temple to Ghatotkacha nearby, in the form of a tree.

Notice the shape of this. In the plains version she is an obstacle in the hero’s path. In the valley she is the presiding goddess. Local tradition consistently promotes the people the epic marginalised, and you have seen the same pattern all the way along this road: the Pandavas in Kinnaur, Draupadi explaining polyandry, Banasura’s head under the temple gate at Sarahan, Yudhishthira’s chariot at Chandratal. The great pan-Indian epic gets localised, and in the localisation the local people become the protagonists.

Kullu

Below Manali, the Kullu valley is the Valley of the Gods — several hundred village deities, each with a palanquin, oracles and a following. Once a year, at Kullu Dussehra, they are carried to Dhalpur maidan in Kullu town to attend on Raghunath, the valley’s presiding form of Rama, and the gathering is one of the great religious spectacles in India. It happens in October, just after you leave.

This is the same deota system you met in Kinnaur on Day 2. It runs the length of the route.

The Beas, and a different kind of valley

The Kullu valley is the anti-Spiti. It is wet, forested, fertile, densely populated, and rich. The Beas rises at Beas Kund above Manali and runs south with orchards on both banks. The apple that Stokes introduced at Kotgarh is the backbone of the economy here too.

It is also, and there is no polite way to say this, one of the most heavily and badly developed valleys in the Himalaya. Concrete hotels stand in the riverbed. The 2023 floods, which tore out hotels, bridges and long stretches of highway, were substantially a lesson in what happens when you build on a floodplain in a warming climate. You will see the repairs.

Old Manali and Vashisht

Old Manali, up the hill from the town, is the older village — timber houses, slate roofs, and the Manu temple. It has been a backpacker centre since the 1970s hippie trail, and it retains a distinct atmosphere.

Vashisht, across the river, has hot sulphur springs in a stone temple complex, dedicated to the sage Vashishtha. After nine days of no hot shower and a night at −8 °C, an hour in a natural hot spring is not a cultural experience, it is a medical intervention. Go.

Naggar: the best thing near Manali

Twenty kilometres south, at Naggar, are two things worth a half-day.

Naggar Castle, a fifteenth-century Kullu royal residence in the local kath-kuni style — stone and cedar, dry-laid, exactly the technique you learned to recognise in Interlude Two, here at palace scale. It is now a hotel and you can walk in.

The Roerich estate. Nicholas Roerich — Russian painter, designer of the sets for Stravinsky’s Rite of Spring, archaeologist, mystic, and the author of an international pact for the protection of cultural property in wartime — settled here in 1928 with his family and lived here until his death in 1947. He painted the Himalaya obsessively: hundreds of canvases in flat, saturated, almost heraldic colour, mountains as pure form.

His son George Roerich, the Tibetologist, is the man who described the Pin Valley buchen ceremony in 1932 (Interlude Three). The family’s Himalayan Research Institute, Urusvati, was based here.

It is a beautiful house with a gallery, a view up the valley, and almost no crowds. It is the best art you will see on this entire trip, and the fact that nobody on the Spiti circuit goes there is a small tragedy.

Kullu Dussehra

If you were three weeks later, you would see the single most extraordinary religious event in the western Himalaya.

Every October, after Dussehra has finished everywhere else in India, the Kullu valley’s village deities — several hundred of them, carried in their palanquins from villages across the district, some walking for days with their retinues of musicians and attendants — converge on the Dhalpur maidan at Kullu town. They come to pay respects to Raghunath, the valley’s presiding form of Rama, whose image was brought here in the seventeenth century after a raja, seeking expiation for a grave sin, installed Rama as the nominal ruler of Kullu and made himself the god’s regent.

For a week the maidan holds a parliament of gods. Deities meet, are carried to visit one another, precede or follow each other in a strictly negotiated order of precedence, and occasionally have disputes about it. Oracles speak. The noise is enormous.

This is the deota system of Interlude One at full scale, and it is not a heritage performance — it is the actual functioning religious politics of the valley, held in public.

What else is nearby, if you have days

  • The Great Himalayan National Park, a UNESCO World Heritage site, southeast of Kullu — genuinely wild, and one of the best-protected pieces of western Himalayan forest.
  • Malana, a village with a famously distinctive social system, its own governing council under the deity Jamlu, and unusual rules about outsiders touching people and property. It is also the centre of a large illegal cannabis economy, which has substantially reshaped the village, and which you should keep well away from: drug policing in Himachal is aggressive and the penalties are severe.
  • Manikaran in the Parvati valley, hot springs, a Sikh gurdwara and a Hindu temple built together over a geothermal field.
  • Bijli Mahadev, on a ridge above Kullu, where a stone lingam is said to be struck by lightning and shattered periodically, and reassembled by the priest with butter.

The last thing

Somewhere on the descent from Rohtang or out of the tunnel, you will pass the point where the first trees start. Watch for it. Nine days earlier you were in deodar forest above Narkanda, and everything since has been a slow removal of life from the landscape and then its return.

Kinnaur gave you green. Nako took the trees away. Spiti took everything except poplar and willow that people planted by hand. Kunzum took the villages. Chandratal took the electricity, the network and the warmth.

And then in one day you get it all back.

That compression is the reason this route is worth doing in this direction, and it is why coming up from Manali in two days — which is faster, and which most people do — is a completely different and much lesser trip. You earned Spiti. Most people just arrive in it.

Chapter 16

What Is Happening Now

Read on the way home, or on the Chandratal night if you are awake anyway.

Everything in this book so far is history. This chapter is about the thing that will decide what the next fifty years look like in these valleys, and you will have driven straight through the evidence.

The apple line is moving up the mountain

This is the clearest, most legible climate signal in Himachal, and you can see it from the car.

An apple tree needs chilling hours — a cumulative period below about 7 °C in winter — to break dormancy and flower properly in spring. Most commercial varieties need somewhere between 1,000 and 1,600 of them. No chill, no proper flowering, no crop.

Winters in Himachal are warming and snowfall is becoming less reliable and later. So the chilling hours at any given altitude are falling. And the apple belt is climbing.

The numbers are stark. In the 1980s the productive apple zone in Himachal sat around 1,200 to 1,500 metres. By the 2000s it had moved to roughly 1,500 to 2,500 metres. Orchards now exist above 3,500 metres — at Leo in upper Kinnaur, and in Lahaul, and increasingly in Spiti, places that were flatly too cold and dry for apples within living memory.

Follow the consequences.

In the old belt — Kotgarh, Thanedar, Kotkhai, the villages Stokes transformed a century ago — orchards at 5,000 to 6,000 feet are failing to get their chill and growers are being told their trees need to move a thousand feet uphill. Studies have found income from fruit falling substantially in Kullu and Shimla districts over recent decades.

In the new belt — Kinnaur, Lahaul, Spiti — income from apples has risen sharply, and land that grew barley is being planted with orchards.

So the whole thing Chapter 2 described, the Stokes revolution that created the Himachal economy, is physically migrating up the road you are driving, and it will arrive in Spiti at roughly the moment it leaves Kotgarh. You will pass both ends of that process on the same trip.

There is a sting. Pollinator populations — honeybees, butterflies — are reported to be declining at the higher altitudes as conditions change, which means the apples may arrive somewhere that cannot pollinate them.

Water

Spiti’s irrigation is snow and glacier melt delivered by kuhl, and the timing is everything. The traditional system depends on winter snow accumulating, then melting gradually through the growing season.

What is changing: less snow, warmer winters, more precipitation falling as rain, earlier melt. Water that used to arrive in June now arrives in April and is gone. Glaciers that buffered the system are shrinking.

Simultaneously, demand has gone up: green peas need far more water than barley, apple orchards need irrigation, and every new guesthouse has flush toilets and showers.

Kaza has a water problem. So do the high villages. Komic, Langza and Hikkim have very little water to spare in a normal year. Some villages in Spiti and Ladakh have experimented with ice stupas — artificial cones of frozen winter water that melt slowly in spring, an ingenious low-tech idea developed in Ladakh by Sonam Wangchuk.

When you are at a homestay, use less water than you would at home. It is not a gesture. In Spiti it is arithmetic.

The glaciers

Bara Shigri, which you pass on Day 9, has been measured retreating since 1906. Chhota Shigri, just across the valley, is one of India’s benchmark monitored glaciers and the source of much of what is known about Himalayan ice loss. Across the western Himalaya the pattern is consistent: retreat, thinning, and increasing amounts of debris cover.

Two consequences matter here.

Water security. These glaciers feed the Chandra, which feeds the Chenab, which irrigates Punjab and Pakistan. The short-term effect of melting is more water; the long-term effect is much less.

GLOFs. As glaciers retreat they leave lakes dammed by loose moraine. Those dams fail. The Shigri event of the 1830s in Chapter 14 was one version; Kedarnath in 2013 and Sikkim in 2023 were others. The number of such lakes in the Himalaya is increasing.

Add black carbon — soot from vehicles, diesel generators and burning downwind — which settles on snow, darkens it and accelerates melting. Every vehicle on this road, including yours, contributes a small amount.

The mountain is falling down faster

The 2021 Kinnaur landslides, including the catastrophic Nigulsari failure, and the devastating 2023 Himachal monsoon, are not separate incidents. They sit at the intersection of three things:

  1. Geology. Kinnaur was always a shear zone with over-steep slopes.
  2. Intensification. The monsoon is delivering more rain in shorter, more violent bursts. A slope that copes with 100 mm over three days fails under 100 mm in three hours.
  3. Construction. Road widening, tunnelling, blasting and unregulated building have cut into slope toes and loaded slope tops across the state.

Which of the three dominates is genuinely disputed, and the dispute is political. What is not disputed is that the frequency of failures has gone up.

Tourism

Spiti received a few thousand visitors a year in the 1990s. It now receives hundreds of thousands in a season, concentrated into four or five months.

The good version: money stays local, because tribal-area land law means outsiders cannot buy in and most accommodation is homestay or locally owned. Ecotourism collectives have built a genuine homestay network and given snow leopards an economic value that has transformed attitudes towards them. Young Spitians can make a living at home.

The bad version: plastic waste in a valley with no functioning waste management and an ecosystem where nothing decomposes; water stress at exactly the time of year water is scarce; traffic and dust in villages built for footpaths; the replacement of a closed-loop dry-toilet system with flush plumbing; and the standard pattern in which a place that becomes famous for being empty stops being empty.

You are part of this. Some concrete things that actually help:

  • Carry your waste out. All of it, including to Manali if necessary. Bottles, wrappers, batteries, everything. There is no landfill.
  • Refill, don’t buy. A bottle and a means of purification removes a dozen plastic bottles from the valley.
  • Stay in homestays rather than hotels where you can.
  • Use less water.
  • Buy local — seabuckthorn juice, Kinnauri shawls, Spitian handicrafts — rather than trucked-in goods.
  • Don’t buy fossils. Chapter 11.
  • Don’t fly drones over villages and monasteries without asking. It is intrusive and in places it is illegal.
  • Ask before photographing people. Especially monks, especially the elderly, especially children. “Juley, photo?” costs nothing.

And still, people live here

It is easy to write this chapter as a lament, and that would be both patronising and wrong.

Spiti in 1970 had no road to Manali, no electricity, no phone, no college, a life expectancy that would horrify you, and an infant mortality rate to match. Winter meant that a complicated birth or a burst appendix was very likely fatal. Nobody who lived through that is nostalgic about it.

What has happened since — the road, the peas, the tunnel at Rohtang, the mobile tower, the hospital, the money — has made lives longer, warmer, safer and more optional. The costs are real and the people bearing them are aware of them and have their own opinions, which are not uniform and are not yours to supply.

The most useful thing a visitor can do is hold both halves at once, spend money locally, take the rubbish away, and not tell anybody how to live.

Coda

What You Just Drove Through

Nine days, about 800 kilometres, and a set of collisions.

The geological one. India hitting Asia, ongoing, at a couple of centimetres a year. You saw the seabed at 4,400 metres and the road falling off the mountain at Nigulsari, and both are the same event.

The religious one. Shiva’s Kailash at Kalpa and Padmasambhava’s footprint at Nako are about eighty kilometres apart. Between them, at Sarahan, a temple with bronzes from both traditions in the same room. Nothing about this frontier is a line. It is a gradient, and people have lived comfortably inside the gradient for a thousand years.

The political one. Guge, Ladakh, Kullu, Bushahr, the Mongols, the Mughals, the Dogras, the Sikhs, the British, India and China — all of them have wanted this corridor, and Ki Gompa’s stacked, rebuilt, patched-together form is what that looks like in architecture.

The economic one. A thousand years of trade running north over the passes, ended in 1962; and a hundred years of apples running south down the road, begun by an American Quaker at Kotgarh. The valley turned around. Everything now points down the highway.

And one thing that is not a collision: the fact that people live here at all. Komic at 4,587 m, cut off for months, −30 °C in winter, no water to spare. A post office at 4,400 m where a man carries the mail out on foot. Losar with no heating. Barley cut by hand on terraces above a gorge. That is the actual achievement on this road, and the monasteries and the fossils and the lake are all, in the end, footnotes to it.

Drive carefully on the 23rd and the 24th. Wait for the sun.

Appendix B

What Is Alive Out There

A quick field list, because nine days is long enough to see most of this if you are looking.

Mammals

  • Blue sheep / bharal — the commonest large mammal in Spiti. Grey-brown, stocky, on scree slopes above villages, often in herds of twenty or more. Near Kibber and Langza, scan the slopes.
  • Himalayan ibex — big curved ridged horns on the males. Higher and shyer than bharal. Pin Valley and Kibber.
  • Himalayan marmot — fat, ginger-brown, sits upright on its haunches, whistles loudly when alarmed. Around Kibber and the Chandratal meadows. Delightful; do not feed.
  • Snow leopard — present in real numbers around Kibber and Pin, essentially invisible in September.
  • Red fox, Himalayan weasel, pika (small, tailless, rabbit-eared, in scree).
  • Chamurthi horse — the local Spiti breed, small, tough, sure-footed, traded at Lavi for centuries.
  • Yak and dzo (yak-cow hybrid), used for ploughing and haulage.

Birds

  • Lammergeier / bearded vulture — enormous, diamond-shaped tail, rusty-orange underside, soars very close to cliffs. Famous for dropping bones to break them. The best bird on the route.
  • Himalayan griffon — huge, pale, soaring in groups on thermals.
  • Golden eagle, Himalayan buzzard.
  • Yellow-billed and red-billed chough — glossy black crows with bright bills, in noisy acrobatic flocks around every village and pass. You will see hundreds.
  • Himalayan snowcock — big grey partridge on high slopes, heard more than seen.
  • Ruddy shelduck — orange, on Chandratal and Nako lake. Locally called brahminy duck; in Tibetan Buddhist areas they are auspicious and left alone.
  • White-capped and plumbeous redstarts — small, on every stream in Kinnaur.
  • Wallcreeper — grey with astonishing crimson wings, flicking up rock faces like a butterfly. A genuinely exciting sight.
  • Hoopoe, magpies, Himalayan monal (in the forested Kinnaur end — iridescent, spectacular, the Himachal state bird).

Plants

  • Deodar (Cedrus deodara) — the great cedar of the lower route, sacred, and the timber of every kath-kuni building.
  • Chilgoza pine (Pinus gerardiana) — Kinnaur only. See Interlude Three.
  • Poplar and willow — the only trees in Spiti, planted deliberately in rows near water for timber and fodder.
  • Seabuckthorn (Hippophae) — dense thorny orange-berried thickets along river beds. Vitamin C, juice, nitrogen fixing, erosion control. A keystone plant of the cold desert.
  • Juniper (shukpa) — burnt as incense in every monastery and household shrine. The smell of Spiti.
  • Caragana — the spiny grey cushion scrub covering the slopes; the main grazing and fuel plant.
  • Ephedra, Artemisia, and in wet pockets, gentians and blue poppies earlier in the season.
